# Chapter 1 — The deal you’re making with your dog

*(Full text for reviewer — synced from manuscript; chapter notes omitted.)*

## Opening

You bought the book, or borrowed it from someone who owns a dog and opinions. Fair enough. Before we talk about sits, recalls, or the mysterious art of not being dragged into a bush, we need a deal — not with me, with **your dog**.

This chapter is not a trick list. It is the contract: what “good” means in your kitchen, what training can actually change, and why this book will spend a surprising amount of time **training you** — your patience, your plan, your volume knob — so your dog gets a fair shot. If that sounds like self-help wearing a lead clip, good. That is partly the point.

## Section 1 — What “good dog” means in real kitchens and muddy parks

“Good dog” is not a personality award. It is not a dog who never barks, never pulls, never steals toast, and never has a Tuesday. **Good dog**, for our purposes, means a dog who can live safely and happily with ordinary humans: clear expectations, kind teaching, management when teaching is not the right tool, and help when something hurts or scares them.

In the kitchen, good might mean “four paws on the floor while I butter bread without launching a small siege.” In the park, good might mean “you can be a dog — sniff, trot, be weird — and still come back when it matters.” Different days, different bar. That is normal.

UK guidance treats **reward-based training** as the sensible default: teach what you want, pay for it, skip kit and “corrections” that rely on pain or fear. This book follows that line because it works **and** it keeps trust on both sides of the cheese.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “Good dog” is **behaviour you can live with**, not a moral grade. Your mood, your deadline, and your neighbour’s perfect spaniel are not the scoring system.  
- **Do:** Pick one picture of “good enough” **this week**. One room. One walk. One habit. Not a lifetime highlight reel.  
- **Don't:** Call the dog “bad” for being a dog. Shame is not a training plan. A rescue on day nine is not a failure because they are not a puppy on Instagram.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Dogs do what works. They are not plotting against your schedule. They notice what pays off in *their* world. Toast wins, sometimes.  
- **Do:** Let them be dogs within safe rules. Sniffing, resting, and playing belong in a normal life. Training is part of that life, not a courtroom.  
- **Don't:** Expect adult proof from a puppy brain. Punishing fear and calling it teaching is a dead end. Perfection in chaos is not “stubborn.” It is a setup problem.

If the bar feels too high today, **make it easier — then ask again.** Good enough this week is enough to start.

## Section 2 — What training can and can’t fix

Four tools. You will mix them. None is cheating.

- **Training** teaches skills. Sit when asked. Settle on a mat. Come when called. Tolerate handling.  
- **Management** stops trouble while you teach. Baby gates. Leads. Shoes in a cupboard. Quiet walk times.  
- **Veterinary care** rules out pain and illness when behaviour suddenly shifts.  
- **Qualified behaviour help** is for patterns that need a professional eye. Serious fear, aggression, compulsions.

Using a lead so your dog does not rehearse lunging at bikes is **smart**, not failure. Calling the vet because the formerly sociable dog snaps when touched is **kind**, not dramatic.

Training cannot promise a dog who ignores squirrels, never barks at the door, or matches a fictional “balanced” ideal from the internet. It **can** give you a shared language, faster recovery from mistakes, and a household that feels less like a daily argument.

### What messes dogs up (usually us)

Most dogs are not broken. They are responding to a world that keeps changing the rules. The hard bit: **we often stress them out without noticing**. We repeat cues like a car alarm. We train when we are already cross. We skip the gate and shout “leave it” from the sofa. We ask for pavement proof when the skill only lives in the hallway.

**Train the human first.** Notice your side before you turn up the volume on theirs.

Skills also need the right **place**. Teach at **house → garden → outside**. Not because your dog is lazy. Because brains learn in boring rooms before they learn on windy pavements. When the street defeats the kitchen, **make it easier — then ask again.** Move back a step on that ladder.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** If you skip management and skip the vet, you often train **through** a problem you should fix first. Then you feel furious and the dog gets the blame.  
- **Do:** Ask “Teach, manage, or vet/pro?” before you repeat a cue for the fifth time.  
- **Don't:** Drill recall while pain is untreated. Don't “socialise” by flooding. One bad afternoon is not proof you failed as an owner.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Dogs need clarity and time. A hallway skill is not automatic on the high street.  
- **Do:** Build in small, paid steps at home first. The book will show you how to grow from there.  
- **Don't:** Learn that “come” means “optional” because it only gets enforced when you are late and shouting.

## Section 3 — Training the Human

Here is the subtitle joke, and it is true: in most kitchens, **the dog learns faster than the person holding the treats**. You have a job, a phone, maybe small humans, and a mental image of how this was supposed to look. The dog has one job in that moment — figure out what pays — and they are often excellent at it.

*Train the human first* is not a scolding. It is a wink. Before you turn up the volume, before you add “one more rep” through gritted teeth, before you decide the dog is disrespecting you — **check your side of the deal**.

When progress stalls, the heat you feel — embarrassment, impatience, that tight “why won’t you just” feeling — is **information about your plan**, not a verdict on the dog. Too hard. Too long. Wrong moment. Wrong reward. World too loud. Dogs feel that heat too. Tight voices, looming hands, and hurry read as pressure even when you mean well.

The fix is almost always on the human side first: smaller step, better pay, shorter session, or **stop while it is still fun**.

You will hear this line all through the book. Chapter 4 unpacks it properly:

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

Say it when you catch yourself about to repeat a cue like a broken car alarm. It is not magic. It is a pause button for **you**. When you fix the setup instead of blaming the dog, try a quiet **“Good human.”** — a marker for the trainer in the room.

This book is a dog-training guide. It is also, in places, **stealth self-help**: how not to take a bad day out on someone who cannot understand your spreadsheet. We will not call it that on every page. We will give you **Train yourself** sections that tell the truth about frustration — and what to do instead. *Train the human first.* Then ask again, easier.

## Section 4 — Train yourself and train the dog (how the rest of this book works)

From Chapter 4 onward, most skills show up in **two columns**: **Train yourself** (your plan, your patience, your stop rules) and **Train the dog** (what they are learning in that lesson). Each side uses **Know**, **Do**, and **Don't**. You will see the shape again soon. Here is a small preview.

**Topic:** Two kinds of walk — **leisure** and **training**. They are not the same job.

**Leisure walk:** sniffing, pacing, being a dog. You are not running a lesson. You still use safe kit. You still **stop** if things go wrong.

**Training walk:** short and on purpose. **One** simple goal. Loose-lead **teaching** and stop-start rules live in **Chapter 10**. We are not doing that machinery here.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Sniffing on a leisure walk is not “ignoring you.” It is dog email. Your impatience often spikes when you treat every walk like a treadmill.  
- **Do:** Say out loud which walk this is before you leave. Leisure or training. Stick to that choice for that outing.  
- **Don't:** Heel-police a leisure walk and then wonder why the dog looks fried. Don't expect a relaxed sniff-fest on a day you are actually teaching lead skills.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Walks can mean different payoffs. Sometimes freedom to sniff. Sometimes “check in for a treat.” Mixed signals are confusing.  
- **Do:** Learn both modes exist. Training walks stay short and clear. Leisure walks stay dog-shaped.  
- **Don't:** Learn that every outing is a test. Don't learn that pulling is the only way to reach a smell because leisure and training got blurred into one grim lap.

Chapter 10 separates **loose-lead practice** from **everyday sniff walks**. The preview here is the split: your patience and plan on one side, their learning on the other.

## Section 5 — Where the how-to lives (not here)

This chapter is the handshake, not the toolbox. **Chapter 4** gives you the step-by-step teaching habit. **Chapter 12** helps you spot when your dog is not okay — read that **before** the big recall, lead, and door chapters. **Chapter 14** is the full pause button when everything wobbles.

If you forget the chapter numbers, remember the mantra: **make it easier — then ask again.** The how-to catches up when you are ready.

## Section 6 — How to use this book

Read in the order in the chapter map, not only by the number on the spine. Do the teaching chapters in Part II, then **Chapter 12 (stress)** before Part III’s recall, lead, and door skills. You want that lens before real-world pressure.

**If you are worried about something specific:**

- **Fear on walks, freezes, “won’t go”** → start with Chapter 12, then back to skills when ready.  
- **Recall in distractions** → foundations in Chapters 7–8, **Chapter 12**, then Chapter 9.  
- **Stuck, grumpy, everything fell apart** → Chapter 14 and the mantra: *make it easier — then ask again.*

One chapter at a time. **House → garden → outside.** Trust the order inside.

**What we promise:** plain language, reward-based methods, honest limits, and repeated permission to **stop** when you or the dog are not okay.

**What we do not promise:** instant fixes, a dog who ignores biology, or a life free of vets and professionals when things are serious.

## Troubleshooting

- **If you feel defensive reading “train the human”** → You are still the hero of the story. We are targeting **habits**, not your worth. Skip to Section 2 and come back.  
- **If you want a command list right now** → Part II is coming. Skipping ahead is how people end up repeating cues louder. This book is trying to save you from that future.  
- **If your dog is growling, biting, or shutting down** → This chapter is not the fix. Manage safety. See Chapters 12 and 14. Call a qualified professional and/or your vet.

## Stop if

- You or the dog are escalating. Voices up. Dog stiff. White-knuckle on the lead. **Stop.** No training beat is worth that.  
- You are training to vent. Walk away. Water. Human snack. Try tomorrow.  
- Something hurts or frightens the dog and you are not sure why. **Vet first.** Training second.
