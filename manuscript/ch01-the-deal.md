## Opening

You bought the book, or borrowed it from someone who owns a dog and opinions. Fair enough. Before we talk about sits, recalls, or the mysterious art of not being dragged into a bush, we need a deal — not with me, with **your dog**.

This chapter is not a trick list. It is the contract: what “good” means in your kitchen, what training can actually change, and why this book will spend a surprising amount of time **training you** — your patience, your plan, your volume knob — so your dog gets a fair shot. If that sounds like self-help wearing a lead clip, good. That is partly the point.

## Section 1 — What “good dog” means in real kitchens and muddy parks

“Good dog” is not a personality award. It is not a dog who never barks, never pulls, never steals toast, and never has a Tuesday. **Good dog**, for our purposes, means a dog who can live safely and happily with ordinary humans: clear expectations, kind teaching, management when teaching is not the right tool, and help when something hurts or scares them.

In the kitchen, good might mean “four paws on the floor while I butter bread without launching a small siege.” In the park, good might mean “you can be a dog — sniff, trot, be weird — and still come back when it matters.” Different days, different bar. That is normal.

UK guidance on responsible dog ownership treats **reward-based training** as the sensible default: teach what you want, pay for it, avoid methods that rely on pain, fear, or intimidation. Professional codes in force-free training say the same in plainer ethics language — no training need requires hurting or frightening a dog. This book follows that line not because it is fashionable, but because it works **and** it keeps trust on both sides of the cheese.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “Good dog” is **behaviour you can live with**, not a moral grade. Your mood, your deadline, and your neighbour’s perfect spaniel are not the scoring system.  
- **Do:** Notice one realistic picture of “good enough” **this week** — one room, one walk, one habit — instead of a lifetime highlight reel.  
- **Don't:** Call the dog “bad” for being a dog; use shame as a training plan; compare a rescue on day nine to a puppy on Instagram.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Dogs do what works. They are not plotting against your schedule; they are learning what pays off in *their* world (toast wins, sometimes).  
- **Do:** Let them be dogs within safe rules — sniffing, resting, playing — so “training” is part of life, not a courtroom.  
- **Don't:** Expect adult proof from a puppy brain; punish fear and call it teaching; ask for perfection in chaos and call it “stubborn.”

## Section 2 — What training can and can’t fix

**Training** teaches skills: sit when asked, settle on a mat, come when called, tolerate handling. **Management** prevents trouble while you teach — baby gates, leads, putting shoes in a cupboard, walking at quiet times. **Veterinary care** fixes or rules out pain, illness, and some behaviour changes that look like “naughtiness.” **Qualified behaviour support** is for patterns that need professional assessment — aggression, severe fear, compulsions, sudden personality shifts.

None of those is cheating. Using a lead so your dog does not rehearse lunging at bikes is **smart**, not failure. Calling the vet because the formerly sociable dog snaps when touched is **kind**, not dramatic.

Training cannot promise a dog who ignores squirrels, never barks at the door, or matches a fictional “balanced” ideal from the internet. It **can** give you a shared language, faster recovery from mistakes, and a household that feels less like a daily argument.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** If you skip management and skip the vet, you are often training **through** a problem you should remove first — and then wondering why you feel furious.  
- **Do:** Ask “Is this a teach moment, a manage moment, or a vet/pro moment?” before you repeat a cue for the fifth time.  
- **Don't:** Drill recall while pain is untreated; “socialise” by flooding; treat every setback as proof you failed as an owner.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Dogs need clarity and time. A skill learned in the hallway is not automatically known on the high street.  
- **Do:** Build skills in small, paid steps; generalise later on purpose (this book will show you how).  
- **Don't:** Learn that “come” means “optional” because it is only enforced when you are already late and shouting.

## Section 3 — Training the Human

Here is the subtitle joke, and it is true: in most kitchens, **the dog learns faster than the person holding the treats**. You have a job, a phone, maybe small humans, and a mental image of how this was supposed to look. The dog has one job in that moment — figure out what pays — and they are often excellent at it.

*Train the human first* is not a scolding. It is a wink. It means: before you turn up the volume, before you add “one more rep” through gritted teeth, before you decide the dog is disrespecting you — **check your side of the deal**.

When progress stalls, the heat you feel — embarrassment, impatience, that tight “why won’t you just” feeling — is **information about your plan**, not a verdict on the dog. Too hard, too long, wrong moment, wrong reward, world too loud. The fix is almost always on the human side first: smaller step, better pay, shorter session, or **stop while it is still fun**.

You will meet a phrase we use a lot, developed properly in Chapter 4:

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

Say it when you catch yourself about to repeat a cue like a broken car alarm. It is not magic. It is a pause button for **you**. Optional habit: when you fix the setup instead of blaming the dog, a quiet **“Good human.”** — marker for the trainer in the room.

This book is a dog-training guide. It is also, in places, **stealth self-help**: how not to take a bad day out on someone who cannot understand your spreadsheet. We will not call it that on every page. We will give you **Train yourself** sections that tell the truth about frustration — and what to do instead.

## Section 4 — Train yourself and train the dog (how the rest of this book works)

From Chapter 4 onward, almost every skill arrives in **two columns**:

**Train yourself** — what you need to understand, what you do with your hands and voice and schedule, and what **not** to do when you are tired or cross.

**Train the dog** — what the dog is learning in that lesson (association, not obedience theatre), what you are building this session, and mistakes **you** avoid on their behalf.

Each side uses three lines:

- **Know**  
- **Do**  
- **Don't**

Here is a tiny preview, so the shape feels familiar before you meet the teaching loop.

**Topic:** Letting the dog sniff on a walk without you marching like a parade commander.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Sniffing is not “ignoring you”; it is dog email. Your impatience often rises when you confuse a walk with a treadmill.  
- **Do:** Decide what “polite walk” means today — maybe sniffing on a loose lead for five minutes, then one easy focus game — and stick to **one** criterion.  
- **Don't:** Yank every three seconds while muttering; speed up because you’re late and blame the dog for the mood **you** brought.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They learn where attention and movement pay off — tight lead vs loose lead, stop vs go.  
- **Do:** Teach “when the lead is loose, we move; when you check in, I pay” in a quiet street before the exciting park.  
- **Don't:** Learn that pulling is the only way to reach the good smell because you only loosen the lead when they lunge.

Full lead chapters come later, with claims and stop rules. The point here is the **split**: your patience and plan on one side, their learning on the other.

## Section 5 — The loop and the five checks (names only)

You do not need the machinery yet — Chapter 4 installs it. For now, names on the map:

**The teaching loop:** setup → cue → behaviour → mark/reward → release → reset.

**The five checks** (when something stalls): setup, reward, criterion, comfort, context — with **“shrink the world”** when the pavement defeats the hallway.

Chapter 12 (which you will read **before** the big live-skill chapters) deepens **comfort** — reading stress without playing vet. Chapter 14 gathers the checks when everything wobbles.

If you remember nothing else from this section: **easier first, then ask again.**

## Section 6 — How to use this book

**Read in the order in the chapter map**, not only by chapter number on the spine. In particular: after the teaching chapters in Part II, read **Chapter 12 (stress)** before Part III’s recall, lead, and door skills. You need that lens before practising under real-world pressure.

**Problem paths (shortcuts when you are worried):**

| If you are mainly worried about… | Start here, then follow the map |
| --- | --- |
| Fear on walks, freezes, “won’t go” | ch12, then back to skills as ready |
| Recall in distractions | ch07–ch08 foundations, **ch12**, then ch09 (some sections need professional review — the book will say so) |
| Stuck anywhere, grumpy trainer, everything fell apart | Five checks / **ch14** |

One chapter at a time. Use the briefs and evidence notes in the repository if you are co-authoring; if you are a reader with only this book, ignore that sentence and trust the order inside.

**What we promise:** plain language, reward-based methods, honest limits, and repeated permission to **stop** when you or the dog are not okay.

**What we do not promise:** instant fixes, a dog who ignores biology, or a life free of vets and professionals when things are serious.

## Troubleshooting

- **If you feel defensive reading “train the human”** → You are still the hero of the story. We are targeting **habits**, not your worth. Skip to Section 2’s manage/teach/vet split and come back.  
- **If you want a command list right now** → Part II is coming. Skipping to tricks without the loop is how people end up repeating cues louder — and this book is trying to save you from that future.  
- **If your dog is growling, biting, or shutting down** → This chapter is not the fix. Manage safety, see ch12 and ch14, call a qualified professional and/or your vet.

## Stop if

- You or the dog are escalating — voices up, dog stiff, you gripping the lead white-knuckled. **Stop.** No training beat is worth that.  
- You are training to vent. Walk away, water, human snack, try tomorrow.  
- Something hurts or frightens the dog and you are not sure why. **Vet first**, training second.
