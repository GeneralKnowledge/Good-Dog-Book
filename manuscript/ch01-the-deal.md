## Opening

You bought the book, or borrowed it from someone who owns a dog and opinions. Fair enough. Before we talk about **sit**, **come**, and **walking nicely without yanking your arm off**, we need a deal — not with me, with **your dog**.

Most people want the **basics**, done kindly. Not a circus dog. Not internet perfection. If your dog sits when it helps, comes back most of the time, and walks without daily tug-of-war, you are already winning by real-life standards. This book is for you.

The subtitle is a joke, and it is also the plot: **Training the Human**. In most kitchens the dog learns faster than the person holding the treats. We are not here to turn you into a trainer with a clipboard. We are here to help you notice when **your** hurry, **your** volume, and **your** bad Tuesday are about to become **their** problem — and to do something else instead. If that sounds like self-help wearing a lead clip, good. That is partly the point.

Picture a normal Monday. The kettle is on. Someone cannot find their shoes. The dog has picked this exact moment to remember that the post exists, and that the postman is a long-standing enemy of the household. You say “quiet” once. Then again, louder. Then a third time in a voice your mother would recognise. The dog barks with more conviction, because from their side of the door you have finally joined in.

Nobody in that scene is a bad person or a bad dog. There is just no plan yet. This chapter is about making one — and about the small, slightly embarrassing truth that most of the plan is about you.

## Section 1 — What “good dog” means in real kitchens and muddy parks

“Good dog” is not a personality award. It is not a dog who never barks, never pulls, never steals toast, and never has a Tuesday. **Good dog**, for our purposes, means a dog you can live with safely and happily: clear rules, kind teaching, sensible shortcuts when teaching is not the right tool today, and help when something hurts or scares them.

In the kitchen, good might mean “four paws on the floor while I butter bread without launching a small siege.” On a walk, good might mean “sniff, trot, be a dog — and come back when I call.” Different days, different bar. That is normal.

It also changes with the dog in front of you. A twelve-week puppy who manages two calm minutes on a mat has had a brilliant day. A three-year-old rescue who takes a treat in the garden for the first time has had a brilliant week. An elderly spaniel who ignores the squirrel *once* is a legend. Compare your dog with your dog last month, not with anybody else’s highlight reel.

UK guidance says teach what you want and **pay for it with treats, praise, or play** — not with fear, pain, or intimidation. This book follows that line because it works **and** it keeps trust on both sides of the cheese. Kind methods are not soft methods. They ask more of you, not less: more thought before the session, more patience in it, and more honesty about when to stop.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “Good dog” is **behaviour you can live with**. It is not a moral grade.  
- **Know:** Your mood, your deadline, and your neighbour’s perfect spaniel are not the scoring system.  
- **Do:** Pick one picture of “good enough” **this week**. One room. One walk. One habit.  
- **Don't:** Call the dog “bad” for being a dog. Shame is not a training plan.  
- **Don't:** Judge a rescue on day nine against a puppy on Instagram.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Dogs do what works. They are not plotting against your schedule.  
- **Know:** They notice what pays off in *their* world. Toast wins, sometimes.  
- **Do:** Let them be dogs within safe rules. Sniffing, resting, and playing belong in a normal life.  
- **Don't:** Expect adult manners from a puppy brain.  
- **Don't:** Punish fear and call it teaching. That road ends somewhere nobody wants to be.

If the bar feels too high today, **make it easier — then ask again.** Good enough this week is enough to start.

## Section 2 — The deal: your side and theirs

Every dog and owner already have a deal. Most just never wrote it down. Here is a fair version.

**Your side of the deal:**

- **Be clear.** One word for one thing. Ask once. Show the dog what you want before you expect it on cue.  
- **Be kind.** Pay for what you like. Prevent what you do not like instead of punishing it afterwards.  
- **Be honest.** If it is too hard, make it easier. If it hurts, see the vet. If it is beyond you, call someone qualified.  
- **Be allowed to stop.** You can end any session, any walk, any visit. So can the dog.

**The dog’s side of the deal:**

- They will **try what worked last time**. That is it. That is the whole clause.

It looks lopsided. It is. You are the one with the opposable thumbs, the fridge, the door key, and the calendar. The dog has a nose and a hopeful face. That is why this book spends so much time on you.

### Four tools, not one

When something goes wrong, owners tend to reach for one tool: **more training**. Usually louder. Most problems need a mix of four:

| Tool | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Teach** | Show the dog a new habit and pay for it | Sit at the kitchen door for a treat |
| **Prevent** | Arrange life so the problem cannot happen while you teach | Baby gate across the kitchen while toast exists |
| **Rest** | Give the dog (and you) enough sleep, sniffing, and calm | A quiet afternoon instead of a third park trip |
| **Get help** | Vet for pain or sudden change; qualified professional for fear or aggression | A sore ear can look a lot like “being difficult” |

Preventing is not cheating. A gate across the kitchen is not admitting defeat. It is buying time so the dog is not practising the toast heist forty times a week while you teach something better. Later chapters call this **management** — plain-English version: *stop the wrong thing happening while you teach the right thing.*

### Train yourself

- **Know:** You hold most of the cards. That is a responsibility, not a guilt trip.  
- **Do:** Next time something goes wrong, ask which of the four tools fits. Teach, prevent, rest, or get help.  
- **Don't:** Treat every problem as a training problem. Some are a gate problem. Some are a vet problem.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The dog cannot read the deal. They only learn it from what keeps happening.  
- **Do:** Make the good choice the easy choice. Gates, leads, chews, and quiet rooms all count.  
- **Don't:** Leave the dog to rehearse the thing you are trying to fix.

## Section 3 — Training the Human (the bit that actually matters)

*Train the human first* is a wink, not a scolding. It means: before you turn up the volume, before you add “one more rep” through gritted teeth, before you decide the dog is disrespecting you — **check your side of the deal**.

You know the feeling. The toast is burning. The dog has discovered the postman is a personal insult. You have said “come” four times and your voice is doing that thing your dog definitely notices. That heat in your chest — embarrassment, impatience, the tight “why won’t you **just**” — is **information about your plan**, not a verdict on the dog. Too hard. Too long. Wrong moment. Wrong treat. World too loud. Dogs feel tight voices and looming hands even when you mean well.

**We often stress them out without noticing.** Nagging. Training when we are already cross. No gate at the door. Asking for street-level “come” when they only know living-room “come.” Most dogs are not broken. The humans are just asking in the wrong place, at the wrong time, with the wrong face.

Here is the phrase to say under your breath when you catch yourself about to repeat a cue like a broken car alarm:

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

It is not magic. It is a pause button for **you**. Easier place. Better treat. One simple ask. Shorter go. **Stop while it is still fun.** When you fix the setup instead of blaming the dog, try a quiet **“Good human.”** — a treat for the trainer in the room.

### Spotting your own warning signs

Dogs give off stress signals. So do owners. Learn yours, because they arrive before your patience runs out:

- Your voice gets louder or higher.  
- You say the same word three times.  
- You start counting reps out loud like a PE teacher.  
- You catch yourself thinking “he knows this” or “she’s doing it on purpose.”  
- You look around to see if anybody is watching.

Any one of those means the same thing: **the session is over, or about to be.** End it on something easy. A sit they know. A hand touch. A scatter of treats on the grass. Then go and put the kettle on. You have not lost. You have just stopped practising frustration, which is the one thing nobody needs more of.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Your frustration is usually telling you the lesson is too big for today.  
- **Know:** It is not telling you the dog failed you.  
- **Do:** Before you repeat a cue, change **one** thing on your side. Quieter room. Tastier treat. Lead on. Or walk away and try tomorrow.  
- **Do:** Learn your own early warning signs and treat them as a stop signal.  
- **Don't:** Train to vent.  
- **Don't:** Turn a leisure walk into a lead lesson because you are late for work.  
- **Don't:** Skip the vet because blaming “dominance” online is cheaper.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They learn what pays off in *this* situation.  
- **Know:** If nagging is the only thing that ever worked, they will wait for nagging.  
- **Do:** Give them clear wins in easy places first — **house, then garden, then outside.**  
- **Don't:** Teach them that their name means “ignore until the human shouts.”

The rest of the book teaches skills. This chapter teaches **you** — patience, plan, volume knob — so the skills have a fair chance. Chapters 4–6 show the step-by-step habit. Chapter 7 helps you spot when your dog is not okay. Chapter 14 is for the day everything wobbles. You do not need to memorise that now.

## Section 4 — Sit, come, walk nicely (and two kinds of walk)

The big three most owners want:

**Sit** when it helps. **Come** when it matters. **Walk nicely** without arm-dislocating heroics.

Add **lie down** and a dog who can **cope being home alone for a while**, and you have covered most of what real households need. Everything else supports those or helps when life is harder — puppies, rescues, worry on walks. **You are allowed to stop at basics** and still have a good dog.

Walks confuse people because there are **two kinds**, and they are not the same job:

- **Leisure walk** — sniffing, being a dog. You are not teaching lead manners on this outing.  
- **Training walk** — short, on purpose, **one** goal. “Walk nicely” practice lives in **Chapter 11**.

Mix them up and you will think your dog is “stubborn” when really you ran a gym session on a day they needed to catch up on dog email.

A leisure walk can still be on a lead, still be safe, and still include the odd treat for a lovely check-in. The difference is your expectation. On a leisure walk you let the dog lead the sniffing (within reason, and not into the bin). On a training walk you pick one tiny job — five steps with a soft lead, say — practise it for a few minutes, then let the walk turn back into a leisure walk. Many dogs need far more of the first kind than the second.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Sniffing on a leisure walk is not “ignoring you.” It is dog email.  
- **Know:** You will get cross if every walk feels like a performance review.  
- **Do:** Before you leave, say which walk this is. Leisure or training. Stick to it for that outing.  
- **Don't:** Lead-police a sniff walk.  
- **Don't:** Expect chilled sniffing freedom on a day you meant to teach “walk nicely.”

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Walks can mean different payoffs. Freedom to sniff sometimes. A treat for checking in sometimes.  
- **Do:** Keep training walks short. Keep leisure walks dog-shaped.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that pulling is the only way to the good smell because the two walks blurred into one grim lap.

## Section 5 — What you need (less than the pet shop thinks)

You do not need a cupboard full of kit to start. You need:

- **Small, soft treats** your dog likes. Pea-sized. Cheese, chicken, or a good shop-bought training treat. Kibble works for easy stuff at home. Most dogs need something better outside.  
- **A treat pouch or a pocket you do not mind ruining.**  
- **A comfortable harness or flat collar, and a normal lead.** Chapter 11 talks about kit. Nothing that tightens, pinches, sprays, or shocks.  
- **A mat or bed** that can move between rooms.  
- **A baby gate or two.** Possibly the best-value training aid ever sold.  
- **Five minutes.** Not an hour. Five.

And one thing that is not for sale: **permission to stop.** Most good sessions are short. Many of the best ones end before the owner feels “finished”.

## Section 6 — Use this book like a cookbook

You do **not** have to read cover to cover in one go. Most owners should not. Find the chapter you need. **Bookmark it** — sticky note, folded corner, app bookmark, photo of the page.

**What this chapter is not:** a glossary, a wall of trainer jargon, or a front-loaded syllabus. We do not list the step-by-step method here on purpose — you get **attitude and navigation** first. The mechanics live in **Chapter 4** and **Chapter 14** when you need them.

The book has five parts:

- **Part I (Chapters 1–3)** — the deal, how dogs learn, and your kit.  
- **Part II (Chapters 4–6)** — how to teach anything, one step at a time.  
- **Part III (Chapter 7)** — reading stress, before you take skills into the real world.  
- **Part IV (Chapters 8–13)** — real life: name, settle, down, alone time, recall, lead walking, doors, handling, and life stages.  
- **Part V (Chapter 14)** — when it goes wrong, and who to call.

**What we promise:** plain language, kind methods, honest limits, permission to **stop** when you or the dog are not okay.

**What we do not promise:** a perfect dog, instant fixes, or that you never need a vet or professional when things are serious.

**Another book on the shelf?** Optional UK further reading — Dogs Trust articles, ABTC signposts, and puppy-friendly guides such as Steve Mann’s *Easy Peasy Puppy Squeezy* if that voice suits you — lives in **Chapter 14**. Nothing here replaces your vet or a qualified behaviourist when safety or welfare is on the line.

## Your first week (optional)

If you like a plan, here is a gentle one. Skip it if you do not.

1. **Day 1–2:** Watch. No training. Notice when your dog is calm, when they get excited, and when you get cross. Write down one of each.  
2. **Day 3:** Put up one gate or move one temptation. Prevent one thing that keeps going wrong.  
3. **Day 4–5:** Pay for something you already like. Every time the dog lies quietly, drop a treat between their paws without a word.  
4. **Day 6:** Decide what kind of walk each walk is. Leisure or training. Say it out loud if it helps.  
5. **Day 7:** Read Chapter 2 with a cup of tea. You have earned it. *Good human.*

## Troubleshooting

- **If you feel defensive reading “train the human”** → We mean habits, not your worth. You are still the hero of the story.  
- **If you only want sit, come, walk nicely** → Good. Bookmark 8, 10, and 11. Skim the rest when you need it.  
- **If someone else in the house does it differently** → Agree one word for each thing and one rule for the kitchen. Stick it on the fridge. Dogs cope with different people. They struggle with different rules.  
- **If your dog is growling, biting, or shutting down** → This chapter is not the fix. Keep everyone safe. See Chapters 7 and 14. Call your vet and/or a qualified professional.

## Stop if

- You or the dog are escalating. Voices up. Dog stiff. White-knuckle on the lead. **Stop.**  
- You are training to vent. Walk away. Water. Snack. Tomorrow.  
- Something hurts or frightens the dog and you are not sure why. **Vet first.**
