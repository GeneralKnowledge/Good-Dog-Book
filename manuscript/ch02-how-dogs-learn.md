## Opening

You made a deal in Chapter 1: clarity, kindness, honest stops. Now the mechanics — not as a university lecture, but as the thing that explains why your dog “knows” sit in the kitchen and pretends they have never heard English in the car park.

Dogs are not small humans in fur coats. They are excellent learners of **what works**: which behaviour buys cheese, which buys freedom, which buys nothing but your increasingly theatrical sighing. This chapter retires **stubborn** as a diagnosis and replaces it with something more useful: consequences, timing, and a fair plan on your side of the lead.

## Section 1 — Dogs learn what works — consequences, not lectures

If learning were about moral lectures, every dog who heard “we don’t do that” would be a saint. Learning is about **outcomes**. Behaviour that is followed by something the dog wants tends to happen again. Behaviour that is followed by nothing useful, or by pain and fear, tends to change in ways you may not enjoy — shutdown, avoidance, or a dog who only cooperates when you are looming over them.

That is the plain version of **positive reinforcement**: add something pleasant after a behaviour, and that behaviour becomes more likely. You do not need quadrant homework on page two. You need consistency: **pay for what you want**, often and clearly, especially while a skill is new.

UK welfare guidance and professional force-free codes agree on the practical line: teach with rewards, avoid training that depends on hurting or frightening a dog. Not because dogs are delicate china, but because fear and pain are lousy teachers for living-room life — and they erode the trust your contract depends on.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Reinforcement is really about **your** consistency. The dog is running experiments; you are the vending machine. If the machine is random, they keep pressing buttons — including the ones you hate.  
- **Do:** Pay for what you want **when it happens**, especially in early learning. One clear consequence beats a paragraph of explanation.  
- **Don't:** Nag like a lecture and call it training; repeat a cue five times hoping volume will install meaning; assume the dog “understands” because you explained it to your partner.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Behaviour follows payoff. “Sit” is not a vow of obedience; it is a movement that has, in the past, produced good things.  
- **Do:** Let them repeat what got paid — that is learning, not defiance when you stop paying.  
- **Don't:** Expect moral understanding, guilt, or respect for your schedule; punish fear and call it teaching.

## Section 2 — Markers, rewards, and the gap between behaviour and emotion

Rewards can be food, play, access (“go sniff”), or a calm moment of relief. The **marker** — a click or a short word like “yes” — is different: it is a camera shutter on a behaviour. It says *that*, right there, was the thing. Then the reward arrives a moment later.

Timing matters more than eloquence. Mark late because you were on your phone, and you may accidentally pay for a different behaviour — the head turn, the butt wiggle, the “I’m giving up” slump. Mark while the dog is still doing the thing you want, then deliver the treat.

Here is the welfare-sensitive bit: **emotion is not disobedience**. A dog who will not sit in the vet waiting room may be scared, not stubborn. A dog who freezes when a child hugs them is not “dominant”; they are telling you the world is too much. Separate “has not learned yet” from “cannot feel safe yet.” Training through shutdown — blank stare, lip lick, creeping away — teaches the wrong lesson: *compliance at the cost of me*.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A marker is precision for **you**, not a magic spell. One system, charged before you ask for real work (Chapter 3).  
- **Do:** Mark the instant the behaviour you want appears; then pay. One marker, one pay — no click storms.  
- **Don't:** Mark late and blame the dog; train through obvious stress because the book said “sit”; confuse “bored” with “overwhelmed.”

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The marker predicts pay; emotion can block learning even when the food is fancy.  
- **Do:** Learn in contexts where success is likely; let scared mean **simplify or manage**, not push.  
- **Don't:** Learn that hands approaching mean intimidation; rehearse failure in chaos and call it proofing.

## Section 3 — Individual differences and realistic timelines

Your neighbour’s spaniel is not your dog’s report card. Breed tendencies, age, health, sleep, and history all change the tempo. A rescue on week two is not a blank slate; a teenager is not a broken version of the puppy you remember. Comparison breeds impatience — and impatience is how people start drilling, nagging, or “just one more” until everyone hates the kitchen.

Realistic timelines are boring and kind: **small steps**, many paid reps, generalisation later on purpose (Chapters 5 and 6). Socialisation is not a conveyor belt of overwhelming novelty; it is careful exposure where the dog can stay curious or calm enough to learn. Flooding — dragging them into the scary thing until they stop struggling — is not toughness. It is a trust withdrawal with interest.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** One dog, one plan. TikTok timelines are edited; your Tuesday is not.  
- **Do:** Celebrate modest wins; adjust criteria when progress stalls (you will get a formal checklist in Chapter 4).  
- **Don't:** Timeline from someone else’s highlight reel; flood to “get it over with”; train angry because development is inconvenient.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Breed, age, and history matter — not as excuses, as **design inputs**.  
- **Do:** Succeed in easy places first; grow difficulty in one dimension at a time.  
- **Don't:** Learn that the world is always too loud; rehearse panic as the price of walks.

## Practice lab — 60-second “what got rewarded?”

No new skill. In the garden or living room, watch your dog for one minute **without** giving cues. Notice what they do and what the environment pays: sniffing, barking at a sound, jumping when you move, settling on the rug. Ask: *what got rewarded — by me, by the room, by the cat?* Write one line. That line is tomorrow’s management or training hint.

## Troubleshooting

- **“He knows it — he’s just being stubborn.”** → Check payoff and context. “Knowing” in the hallway is not knowing on the pavement. Stubborn is a story; consequences are the data.  
- **“She only works for food.”** → Food is a salary. You can diversify pay later; early learning is allowed to be edible.  
- **“He shuts down when I train.”** → Softer setup, higher-value pay, shorter sessions — or stop and read Chapter 12’s comfort lens. Shutdown is not laziness.

## Stop if

- The dog is showing stress **clusters** (won’t eat, stiff, hiding, trembling) — simplify, manage, or pause. Training is not emergency dentistry.  
- You are repeating cues like a car alarm. Stop. Breathe. Chapter 4’s mantra is coming; use it early: **make it easier — then ask again.**  
- You suspect pain or illness — vet before more reps.
