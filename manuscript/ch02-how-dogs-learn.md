## Opening

You made a deal in Chapter 1: clarity, kindness, honest stops. Now the mechanics — not as a university lecture, but as the thing that explains why your dog “knows” sit in the kitchen and pretends they have never heard English in the car park.

Dogs are not small humans in fur coats. They are excellent learners of **what works**: which behaviour buys cheese, which buys freedom, which buys nothing but your increasingly theatrical sighing. This chapter retires **stubborn** as a diagnosis and replaces it with something more useful: consequences, feelings, timing, and a fair plan on your side of the lead.

Chapter 1 gave you the map: **sit, come, walk nicely**, bookmarks welcome, plain habits first. This chapter stays in plain language — payoffs and timing, not trainer homework. If you only read one idea from it, make it this: **your dog is always learning, whether or not you think you are training.**

## Section 1 — Dogs learn what works — consequences, not lectures

If learning were about moral lectures, every dog who heard “we don’t do that” would be a saint. Learning is about **outcomes**. Behaviour that is followed by something the dog wants tends to happen again. Behaviour that is followed by nothing useful, or by pain and fear, tends to change in ways you may not enjoy — shutdown, avoidance, or a dog who only cooperates when you are looming over them.

That is the plain version of **positive reinforcement**: add something pleasant after a behaviour, and that behaviour becomes more likely. You do not need diagrams on page two. You need consistency: **pay for what you want**, often and clearly, especially while a skill is new.

UK welfare guidance and professional ethics agree on the practical line: teach with rewards, avoid training that depends on hurting or frightening a dog. No everyday training need requires pain, fear, or intimidation. Not because dogs are delicate china, but because fear and pain are lousy teachers for living-room life — and they erode the trust your deal depends on.

### The dog decides what counts as a reward

Here is the bit that catches people out. **You** do not decide what is rewarding. The dog does. A pat on the head is lovely to some dogs and mildly annoying to others. A dry biscuit is fine in the kitchen and worthless next to a fox smell. “Good boy” in a warm voice is pleasant; it is rarely enough to beat a squirrel.

If a behaviour is not getting more frequent, your “reward” may not be one. That is not a judgement on the dog’s gratitude. It is information. Chapter 3 helps you build a **reward menu** so you have something that works in the place you are actually standing.

### The world pays too

You are not the only one handing out rewards. The world does it all day long:

- Jumping up gets a face to lick, and your hands on them. Paid.  
- Barking at the window makes the postman leave. Every single time. Paid.  
- Pulling on the lead gets them to the lamppost. Paid.  
- Pinching a sock starts the best chase game in the house. Very paid.

Nobody planned any of those lessons. They happened anyway. That is why so much of this book is about **prevention** — arranging life so the unhelpful thing does not keep paying while you teach something better. Shut the front-room curtains. Pick the socks up. Turn into a statue when the dog jumps, then pay four paws on the floor.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Learning is really about **your** consistency. The dog is running experiments. You are the vending machine.  
- **Know:** If the machine is random, they keep pressing buttons — including the ones you hate.  
- **Do:** Pay for what you want **when it happens**, especially in early learning.  
- **Do:** Look for what the world is paying for. Then block that payoff where you can.  
- **Don't:** Nag like a lecture and call it training.  
- **Don't:** Repeat a cue five times hoping volume will install meaning.  
- **Don't:** Assume the dog “understands” because you explained it to your partner.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Behaviour follows payoff. “Sit” is not a vow of obedience. It is a movement that has, in the past, produced good things.  
- **Do:** Let them repeat what got paid. That is learning, not defiance when you stop paying.  
- **Don't:** Expect moral understanding, guilt, or respect for your schedule.  
- **Don't:** Punish fear and call it teaching.

## Section 2 — Dogs also learn how things feel

There is a second kind of learning running quietly underneath the first. Dogs learn what predicts what — and **how they feel about it.**

The lead coming off the hook predicts a walk, so the lead makes them bounce. The rustle of the cheese packet predicts cheese, so they appear from three rooms away. The car predicts the vet, so the car makes some dogs tremble. Nobody taught any of that on purpose. The dog simply noticed that one thing reliably comes before another, and their feelings followed.

This is useful in two ways.

First, it explains a lot of “random” behaviour. A dog who hides when you pick up the nail clippers is not being dramatic. They have learned what the clippers predict. A dog who goes wild when you put your shoes on has learned the shoes mean adventure.

Second, you can use it kindly. If something worries your dog, you can pair it — at a distance they can cope with — with something wonderful, until the scary thing starts to predict good news. The brush appears, chicken appears. The doorbell rings (quietly, on a recording), cheese rains from the sky. Done gently and slowly, the feeling changes, and behaviour often changes with it. Chapters 7, 11, and 12 use this idea for harnesses, visitors, and handling.

Done badly — too close, too loud, too fast — the opposite happens. The chicken starts to predict the scary thing. That is why the rule is always **at a distance the dog can cope with**, and why you stop if they stop eating.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Your dog is learning associations all day. Your keys, your shoes, your sighs, your “come here” voice.  
- **Do:** Notice what your dog gets excited or worried about before anything happens. That tells you what they have learned to predict.  
- **Don't:** Use the thing they love to trick them into the thing they fear. Cheese is not a bribe for being grabbed.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Feelings come first. A worried dog is not choosing to ignore you.  
- **Do:** Pair worrying things with good things, at a distance that keeps them comfortable.  
- **Don't:** Rush to close the gap. If they stop taking food, you were too close or too fast.

## Section 3 — Markers, rewards, and the gap between behaviour and emotion

Rewards can be food, play, access (“go sniff”), or a calm moment of relief. The **marker** — a click or a short word like “yes” — is different. It is a camera shutter on a behaviour. It says *that*, right there, was the thing. Then the reward arrives a moment later.

Timing matters more than eloquence. Mark late because you were on your phone, and you may accidentally pay for a different behaviour — the head turn, the bottom wiggle, the “I’m giving up” slump. Mark while the dog is still doing the thing you want, then deliver the treat.

Why bother with a marker at all? Because treats are slow. By the time your hand has found the pouch, fumbled the cheese, and reached the dog, three other things have happened. The marker bridges that gap. It tells the dog exactly what earned the treat, even when the treat arrives a second later.

Here is the welfare-sensitive bit: **emotion is not disobedience**. A dog who will not sit in the vet waiting room may be scared, not stubborn. A dog who freezes when a child hugs them is not “dominant”. They are telling you the world is too much. Separate “has not learned yet” from “cannot feel safe yet.” Training through shutdown — blank stare, lip lick, creeping away — teaches the wrong lesson: *go along with it, whatever it costs me*.

A useful test: **would they eat their favourite treat right now?** If not, they are probably too worried, too excited, or too unwell to learn. That is not a training problem. That is a “make it easier, or stop” moment.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A marker is precision for **you**, not a magic spell. One system, charged before you ask for real work (Chapter 3).  
- **Do:** Mark the instant the behaviour you want appears. Then pay.  
- **Do:** One marker, one treat. No click storms.  
- **Don't:** Mark late and blame the dog.  
- **Don't:** Train through obvious stress because the book said “sit”.  
- **Don't:** Confuse “bored” with “overwhelmed”.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The marker predicts a treat.  
- **Know:** Strong feelings can block learning even when the food is fancy.  
- **Do:** Learn in places where success is likely.  
- **Do:** Let “scared” mean **simplify or prevent**, not push.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that approaching hands mean intimidation.  
- **Don't:** Let them rehearse failure in chaos while you call it practice.

## Section 4 — What happens when you stop paying

Sooner or later you will meet this. A behaviour that used to pay stops paying — and for a while, it gets **worse**, not better.

You decide to stop feeding the dog from the table. Night one, they stare. Night two, they stare and whine. Night three, they put a paw on your knee and sing the song of their people. This is normal. When something that always worked suddenly does not, most animals (humans included — think of a lift button that will not light) try **harder** before they give up.

If you cave on night three, you have just taught the dog that singing works. Worse, you have taught them that singing works *eventually*, which makes it very persistent indeed.

The kind way through is not to grit your teeth and ignore a frantic dog for an hour. It is to:

1. **Prevent** the old payoff (dog on a mat with a chew while you eat, or a gate).  
2. **Pay generously** for the new thing (lying on the mat).  
3. Expect a short, noisy wobble — and do not panic when it comes.

If the dog seems genuinely distressed rather than just hopeful, that is different. Stop, make it easier, and read Chapter 7.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A short burst of “worse” often means your plan is working.  
- **Do:** Decide in advance what you will pay for instead. Have the treats ready.  
- **Don't:** Give in on the third whine. That teaches the dog that whining harder works.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They are not testing your authority. They are testing whether the old rule still works.  
- **Do:** Give them a clear alternative that pays well.  
- **Don't:** Leave them with nothing to do while the old payoff disappears.

## Section 5 — Individual differences and realistic timelines

Your neighbour’s spaniel is not your dog’s report card. Breed tendencies, age, health, sleep, and history all change the tempo. A rescue in week two is not a blank slate. A teenager is not a broken version of the puppy you remember. Comparison breeds impatience — and impatience is how people start drilling, nagging, or “just one more” until everyone hates the kitchen.

Some dogs were bred to chase, some to sniff, some to guard, some to work all day beside a person. You do not need a breed encyclopaedia. You just need to notice what your dog finds easy and what they find very hard, and plan around it. A scent hound who struggles with recall in a field full of rabbit smells is not failing you. They are doing what generations of their family were selected to do. Your job is to make coming back worth more than the rabbit — and to use a long line until it is.

Realistic timelines are boring and kind: **small steps**, many paid repetitions, generalisation later on purpose (Chapters 5 and 6). Most everyday skills take weeks of short sessions, not an afternoon. Socialisation is not a conveyor belt of overwhelming novelty. It is careful exposure where the dog can stay curious or calm enough to learn. Flooding — dragging them into the scary thing until they stop struggling — is not toughness. It is a trust withdrawal with interest.

Sleep matters more than people expect. Adult dogs sleep a lot. Puppies and young dogs need a great deal more. A tired dog learns badly and copes badly, a bit like a toddler at a wedding reception. If training has fallen apart this week, check the sleep before you check the method.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** One dog, one plan. Social media timelines are edited. Your Tuesday is not.  
- **Do:** Celebrate modest wins.  
- **Do:** Adjust the plan when progress stalls. You will get a checklist in Chapter 4.  
- **Don't:** Set your timeline from someone else’s highlight reel.  
- **Don't:** Flood to “get it over with”.  
- **Don't:** Train angry because development is inconvenient.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Breed, age, sleep, and history matter. Not as excuses — as **design inputs**.  
- **Do:** Succeed in easy places first.  
- **Do:** Grow difficulty in one way at a time.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that the world is always too loud.  
- **Don't:** Make panic the price of a walk.

## A worked example — the dog who jumps up

Put the whole chapter together on one everyday problem.

**What is paying?** Jumping gets eye contact, hands, voices, sometimes a squeal. All of that is lovely to a dog who wants to say hello.

**How does it feel?** The dog is excited. Visitors and you coming home predict fun. That feeling will not vanish because you said “off”.

**What to do instead:**

1. **Prevent** — for now, greet with a gate between you, or with the dog on a lead, so jumping does not pay.  
2. **Teach** — drop a few treats on the floor as you come in. Four paws on the floor is now where the good stuff lives.  
3. **Pay** — mark and treat calm feet, or a sit if they offer one. Bend down to say hello when all four paws are on the floor.  
4. **Expect a wobble** — they may jump harder for a day or two. Stay boring. Keep paying for the floor.

No knees in the chest. No stepping on paws. No shouting. Just a better deal, made obvious.

## Practice lab — 60-second “what got rewarded?”

No new skill. In the garden or living room, watch your dog for one minute **without** asking for anything. Notice what they do and what the room pays them for: sniffing, barking at a sound, jumping when you move, settling on the rug. Ask: *what got rewarded — by me, by the room, by the cat?*

Write one line. That line is tomorrow’s prevention or training idea.

Do it again tomorrow at a different time of day. You may notice that the evening dog and the morning dog are not quite the same animal.

## Troubleshooting

- **“He knows it — he’s just being stubborn.”** → Check payoff and place. “Knowing” in the hallway is not knowing on the pavement. Stubborn is a story. Consequences are the data.  
- **“She only works for food.”** → Food is a salary. You can widen the pay later. Early learning is allowed to be edible.  
- **“He shuts down when I train.”** → Softer setup, tastier treats, shorter sessions — or stop and read Chapter 7. Shutdown is not laziness.  
- **“It was getting better, now it’s worse.”** → Check sleep, health, and whether the old payoff crept back. A short wobble after a change is normal.

## Stop if

- The dog is showing **clusters** of stress signs (won’t eat, stiff, hiding, trembling). Simplify, prevent, or pause.  
- You are repeating cues like a car alarm. Stop. Breathe. **Make it easier — then ask again.**  
- You suspect pain or illness. Vet before more practice.
