# Chapter 9 — Settle, calm, and time alone

## Opening

The doorbell, the kettle, the children cartwheeling past the sofa — home is not a monastery. Yet most households want something simple: a dog who can **switch off** sometimes, without being shouted into stillness or pinned to the floor in the name of calm. And a dog who can cope when everybody has to go out.

**Settle** in this book means **invited rest with pay**, not suppressed alertness. You are teaching a landing pad (often a mat), a rest spot the dog can choose, and — gradually — the idea that being on their own for a while is safe and a bit boring. Short sessions beat marathon “calm down” battles. Welfare guidance favours brief, positive training over endless drilling.

This chapter builds on **down** from Chapter 5. A dog who can lie down easily has a head start on settling.

**Bookmark this chapter** for calm at home and for getting ready to leave your dog alone. You do not need a show-ring settle to have a good pet.

## Section 1 — The mat: a landing pad for calm

A mat is a portable signal: “here is a place where a relaxed body earns gentle rewards.” At first, the dog may stand, sit, or lie on it. You are building **calm on the mat**, not a statue competition. Start in a boring room with no audience.

### Teaching settle on a mat

This follows the approach Dogs Trust uses: quiet, mostly silent, paying for relaxation as it appears.

**You need:** a mat or blanket, a chair for you, very small treats, and a quiet room. A lead on the dog at first can help some bouncy dogs, but is optional.

1. **Sit down and say nothing.** Put the mat on the floor by your chair. Have treats ready.  
2. **Pay any interest in the mat.** Sniffing it, stepping on it — calmly drop a treat on the mat.  
3. **Pay lying down.** If the dog lies down on the mat (they may, if you have taught down), place a treat between their paws. You do not need to ask for down. Let them offer it.  
4. **Pay signs of relaxing.** Watch for small things: a sigh, shifting weight onto one hip, resting their chin on the floor or their paws, eyes softening. Each one earns a slow, quiet treat placed on the mat.  
5. **Release.** Say your free word, stand up, and let them get off. A short sniff around is fine.  
6. **Stop after a few minutes.**

Some dogs lie down quickly. For them, pay only lying down, then only the relaxed signs. Some dogs find it hard — they stare at you, paw at you, or bounce about. For them, start smaller: pay standing still near the mat, looking away from you, or sniffing the mat.

The silence is important. You are not asking them to settle. You are waiting for settling to happen and making it pay. Over time, the dog learns to settle **without being told** — which is what you want when you are on the phone or eating dinner.

### Building time — in honest seconds

Add time in tiny steps. Dogs Trust suggests building up by **a couple of seconds at a time, over several sessions**.

1. **One second** of calm on the mat → treat → release. Repeat until boring.  
2. **Two or three seconds** → treat. If they pop up, they pop up. No scolding. Invite again later.  
3. **Five seconds** — only when shorter waits are easy most of the time. Keep releasing often.  
4. **Several treats, spread out** — while they lie relaxed, place a treat every few seconds, then a bit less often.  
5. **Stop while the mat still looks like a good deal** — three to five minutes total.

If the dog gets up, simply wait for them to settle again before you pay. If they cannot settle at all, the room is too exciting, or you are asking for too long. Make it easier.

### Adding a little life

When the dog will lie relaxed on the mat for a little while in a quiet room, add **one** small extra. Dogs Trust suggests starting with someone walking past, then building up to busier things like sweeping or a game going on nearby.

- You stand up and sit down again.  
- A family member walks slowly across the room.  
- You pick up a book.  
- The kettle goes on next door.

One thing per session. Pay generously when the dog stays relaxed. If they get up, make the distraction smaller or further away.

Then try the mat in a different room. Then in a café garden, much later. A mat that comes with you on days out becomes a little piece of home.

Keep sessions **short**. A handful of successes beats a marathon. If they leave, they leave. Forced pinning teaches nothing useful and erodes trust.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Calm grows in **tiny steps**. Your impatience is the usual reason settle becomes a fight.  
- **Do:** Sit quietly. Let the dog work it out.  
- **Do:** Count successes, not minutes.  
- **Do:** Use your release word so getting off the mat is legal and fun.  
- **Don't:** Train settle while you are cooking a stressful dinner.  
- **Don't:** Punish leaving the mat.  
- **Don't:** Expect adult nap length on day one.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The mat means “good things happen when I relax here.”  
- **Do:** Discover that stillness pays better than pacing.  
- **Do:** Learn a clear end to the exercise.  
- **Don't:** Let “settle” come to mean “trapped until the human is less angry.”

## Section 2 — Calm around movement — honest expectations

Dogs notice movement. Post arrives, curtains twitch, someone booms down the stairs. Alertness is **normal**, not naughtiness. Your goal is not a dog who ignores life. It is a dog who can **recover**, and who is not rehearsing frantic barking every afternoon.

Teach in **small steps**: one person walks past at a distance while you pay calm on the mat. Or pay quiet watching before the dog launches into commentary. If barking already happens, **prevent** it while you teach — baby gate, closed door, window film or a closed curtain on the front window. Do not yell over the noise and call it training.

Pay **recovery**. The moment they soften after a startle is worth a quiet treat. You are paying the return to calm, not punishing the alert.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Calm is a **behaviour you pay for**, not a mood you demand. Television dogs are edited.  
- **Do:** Lower the excitement in the room when practising.  
- **Do:** One movement trigger per session.  
- **Don't:** Punish alertness.  
- **Don't:** Let the dog rehearse chaos every day without prevention.  
- **Don't:** Train through shutdown (see Chapter 7).

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Movement predicts different things in different houses.  
- **Do:** Learn that quiet watching, or softening after a bark, can pay.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that barking is the only way to make the postman disappear.

## Section 3 — Rest spot and choice to leave

Beyond the mat, give the dog a **rest spot** they own. A bed in a quiet corner, a covered crate with the door open, a space behind a gate. The rule: when they are there, **nobody pokes them** — especially children. The dog must also be able to **leave** if they choose. Blocking the exit turns rest into stress.

Teach humans the script. “Dog on bed = we walk past.” Pair this with prevention when visitors or chaos are more than your dog can handle today. Settle skills support the door and guest chapter later. They are not a substitute for gates and leads when excitement spikes.

### How much rest does a dog need?

More than most people think. Adult dogs typically spend a big part of the day resting or asleep. Puppies, teenagers, older dogs, and dogs settling into a new home often need even more. An overtired dog can look hyper, mouthy, and “naughty”, a bit like a toddler at a wedding.

If your dog never seems to switch off, try building in **quiet time** after walks and play: a chew in the rest spot, curtains half closed, the house a bit calmer. Many owners are surprised how much easier their dog becomes.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Choice reduces the risk of bites and burnout.  
- **Know:** Forced cuddles on the rest spot undo the lesson.  
- **Do:** Put a family rule on the fridge.  
- **Do:** Supervise children with the dog until settle is reliable.  
- **Don't:** Drag the dog to the bed for a photo.  
- **Don't:** Train when the dog is already over the top.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The rest spot means predictable quiet and escape from hassle.  
- **Do:** Choose to rest there when tired. Trust that leaving is allowed.  
- **Don't:** Let beds become places where they get grabbed or bothered.

## Section 4 — Time alone: helping your dog cope when you go out

Sooner or later, everybody has to leave the dog. Work, shopping, school run, a night out. Most dogs can learn to cope with being alone for a sensible length of time — **if they are taught gradually**. Many struggle more than their owners realise. The RSPCA reports research suggesting that around **eight in ten dogs** find being left alone hard, and that about half of those show no obvious signs.

This section is about **prevention**: teaching a dog that being alone is safe and a bit boring, before problems start. If your dog is already distressed when left — more on the signs below — this is not a DIY fix. Please read “When to get help” at the end of the section.

### Start before you need it

Dogs Trust advises starting to teach time alone **as early as possible**, ideally while the dog is still a puppy, and for rescue dogs once they have had a few days to settle in. Do not wait until the day you go back to the office.

### The building blocks

These all happen **while you are still at home**:

- **A comfy rest spot** (Section 3) where the dog likes to be.  
- **Settling on a mat** while you are busy nearby (Section 1).  
- **Baby gates** so the dog can be in a different room while still seeing, hearing, and smelling you.  
- **Long-lasting chews and food toys** — a stuffed food toy, a lick mat, a safe chew — that the dog enjoys on their own. Take the food from their daily allowance.  
- **Not following you everywhere.** If your dog shadows you to the bathroom, start paying them for staying on their bed while you pop out of the room and straight back.

### Step by step

Go at the dog’s pace. Repeat each step until the dog is relaxed before moving on. Some dogs move through these in days. Some take weeks. Both are normal.

1. **Settle with you nearby.** Ask the dog to go to their bed or mat. Sit close by. Pay them, quietly, for staying relaxed.  
2. **Move away a little.** Get up and walk a few steps away. Come back. If they stayed calm on the bed, pay them there.  
3. **Further and longer.** Gradually go further across the room, then out of the room, for a few seconds, then a little longer. If the dog gets up and follows, or looks worried, you went too far too fast. Go back a step. No telling off.  
4. **Behind a gate.** Give the dog a long-lasting chew or food toy, step through a baby gate, and potter about where they can still see you. Start with a minute or two. If they seem worried, stay closer to the gate. Build up slowly.  
5. **Out of sight.** Close a door between you for a few seconds while they enjoy their chew. Come back calmly. Then a little longer.  
6. **Out of the house.** Go out of the front door and come straight back in. Then go out and stay out for a minute. Then a few minutes. Start varying how long you are gone — sometimes short, sometimes a bit longer — so leaving does not always mean a long absence.  
7. **Real outings.** Once the dog is relaxed for up to an hour or so, longer periods often become easier. Keep giving them something enjoyable to do when you go.

Dogs Trust suggests **a few minutes is enough to start with** for the first real absences. Build up very gradually.

### Before you leave

Set the dog up to succeed:

- **Exercise and toilet first.** A walk and a sniff earlier on, then a calm half-hour before you go, so they are ready to rest.  
- **Water, and something to do.** A food toy or long-lasting chew. Some dogs like an old jumper that smells of you, or the radio on quietly.  
- **A safe space.** Move anything precious or dangerous out of reach. Close off rooms you don’t want them in.  
- **Low-key leaving.** Get ready so you can go quickly and calmly. No long, emotional goodbyes. A short, ordinary phrase like “see you later” can become a reassuring routine.  
- **Low-key coming home.** Say hello calmly. If there has been an accident or something is chewed, let the dog out into the garden before you clear up. Then clear up quietly.

### Never punish what happened while you were out

This one matters. If you come home to a chewed cushion or a puddle, **do not tell the dog off**. They cannot connect your anger to something that happened an hour ago. What they learn is that your coming home is unpredictable and scary — which can make them **more** anxious about being left, and make the problem worse.

That “guilty look” people describe? It is the dog reacting to *your* face and voice right now. It is not guilt about the cushion.

### How long is too long?

Both Dogs Trust and the RSPCA advise that adult dogs should **not be left alone for more than about four hours**. Puppies, older dogs, and dogs still learning to cope need much shorter absences. Never leave a dog for so long that they become distressed.

If your days are longer than that, plan for it: a dog walker, a friend or neighbour who pops in, or a good doggy day care. Ask your dog to cope with what they can cope with — not with what your diary says.

### Signs your dog is not coping

A phone or camera pointed at the dog while you are out is the easiest way to find out how they really feel. Watch for:

- barking, whining, or howling;  
- pacing, panting, trembling, or drooling;  
- scratching or chewing at doors, windows, or the things nearest the exit;  
- toileting indoors when they are normally clean;  
- not touching their food toy until you come back;  
- being unable to settle, even for a short while;  
- before you leave: following you, getting between you and the door, trembling when the keys or coat come out.

A dog who eats their chew, has a wander, and goes to sleep is coping. A dog who cries at the door for the whole time is not, even if nothing gets chewed.

If you see signs of worry during training, **go back a step and go more slowly**. That is the whole fix at the prevention stage.

### What does not help

- **Leaving them to “cry it out.”** A distressed dog does not learn to be calm by being more distressed.  
- **Shutting a worried dog in a crate.** A crate can be a cosy den for a dog who already loves it. But Dogs Trust warns that a dog who panics in a crate can seriously injure themselves. A crate does not cure worry about being alone.  
- **Getting another dog to keep them company.** Dogs Trust does not recommend this as a fix. Often you just end up with two dogs who struggle.  
- **Over-the-counter calming products as the plan.** Talk to your vet instead.

### When to get help

If your dog is already showing distress when left, the most important thing, while you get help, is to **avoid leaving them alone for longer than they can cope with**. Dogs Trust describes this as the only way to stop the problem getting worse. Ask family, friends, a dog sitter, or day care to help bridge the gap.

Then:

- **See your vet** — especially if the change is recent, if the dog seems more fearful in general, or if they are suddenly clingier. Pain and illness can play a part. Your vet can also refer you on.  
- **Contact a qualified clinical animal behaviourist** (Chapter 14 explains how to find one in the UK). Separation-related problems are very treatable, but they usually need a proper plan made for your dog.  
- **Dogs Trust** runs a free behaviour support line for owners. Check their website for current details.

Distress when left alone is **not** spite, revenge, or a dog who is “spoiled”. It is a dog who is frightened. Treat it that way, and you are already doing the most important thing.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Coping alone is a skill. It is taught, gradually, like everything else.  
- **Know:** Coming home to chaos is upsetting. Your dog was upset first.  
- **Do:** Start practising long before you need to leave for real.  
- **Do:** Keep leaving and coming home calm and boring.  
- **Do:** Film the dog if you are not sure how they are doing.  
- **Don't:** Punish anything that happened while you were out.  
- **Don't:** Leave an adult dog for more than about four hours.  
- **Don't:** Push on when the dog shows signs of worry. Go back a step.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Being alone can be safe, predictable, and a bit boring.  
- **Do:** Learn that your going out is followed by something nice to do, and that you always come back.  
- **Don't:** Let them practise panic. If they cannot cope, change the plan, not the dog.

## Practice lab A — Mat settle, three minutes max

**You need:** a quiet room, a mat, a chair, small treats. Nobody practising cartwheels nearby.

**Try this:**

1. **Sit quietly.** Pay any interest in the mat. Three to five treats.  
2. **Pay calm.** Any lying down, sigh, or chin-rest on the mat earns a slow treat between the paws.  
3. **Release** with your free word. Let them go and sniff.  
4. **Optional stretch** — if calm came easily, try waiting **two seconds** longer once. Then release and end.

**Look for:** a softer body, slower breathing, maybe a sigh.

**If it’s not working:** go back to paying contact with the mat, or try a smaller mat in a duller corner. **Make it easier — then ask again.**

**Stop if:** the dog won’t eat, is panting, or you hear yourself repeating “settle”. Put the mat away. Try tomorrow.

## Practice lab B — Two minutes behind the gate

For dogs who are not yet showing distress when left. If your dog already struggles, skip this and read “When to get help” above.

**You need:** a baby gate between two rooms, a long-lasting chew or stuffed food toy.

**Try this:**

1. After a walk, settle the dog on their side of the gate with the chew.  
2. Step to your side. Potter about where they can still see you — fold washing, make tea.  
3. After **two minutes**, if they are still happily chewing, step back through calmly. No big hello.  
4. Repeat on another day. Add a minute at a time when it is easy.

**Look for:** a dog who keeps chewing and maybe glances at you, then carries on.

**If it’s not working:** stay closer to the gate, or try for one minute. Some dogs need you sitting right by the gate at first.

**Stop if:** the dog leaves the chew, cries, scrabbles at the gate, or pants. Go back to an easier step next time.

## A note on “calm down” language

Owners often say **calm down** when they mean **stop bothering me**. The dog hears volume and tension, not a taught skill. Swap the phrase for something you have actually trained — mat, rest spot, quiet praise for softening — and prevent problems when you cannot teach. Forced stillness (holding, pinning, shouting until quiet) may produce silence while stress rises. Chapter 7’s comfort check catches that mismatch.

## Troubleshooting

- **Settle only works with you in the room** → Normal early on. Add short exits, come back, pay calm. One change at a time (Chapter 5).  
- **The dog erupts when the doorbell goes** → Prevention first. Settle is parallel work, not the door fix (Chapter 12).  
- **They won’t settle at all** → Vet check (pain, itch, sleep). Shorter sessions. Check the treat is worth it.  
- **The dog is fine alone some days and not others** → Think stress bucket (Chapter 7). A busy or scary day makes being alone harder. Shorter absences after hard days.  
- **The dog destroys things only when alone** → Could be boredom, could be worry. Film them. If they look relaxed and busy, give more to do and more exercise. If they look worried, see “When to get help”.

## Stop if

- Growling when approached on the rest spot. Stop. Give space. See Chapter 14.  
- Panting, shaking, or refusing food on the mat in a “quiet” room. The comfort check failed. Simplify or stop.  
- You are using the mat as punishment (“go to your place” after shouting). Pause. Rebuild with invited, paid calm.  
- Your dog shows distress when left alone. Stop building time. Keep absences within what they can cope with. Get help.

## Before the next chapter

Home calm makes **recall and lead work** easier — a dog who can recover indoors copes better outdoors. Chapter 10 tackles coming back when it matters. Bring your mat mindset: trust is built in small, cheerful payments, not in one heroic afternoon.

If your household is chaotic today, settle is still worth one **micro-session** — thirty seconds on the mat with a single treat — so both of you remember what calm feels like before the week runs away from you.
