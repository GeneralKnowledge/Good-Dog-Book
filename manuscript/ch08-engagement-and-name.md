## Opening

Recall, polite walks, and door chaos all lean on the same humble skill: your dog **choosing to turn towards you** when the world is merely interesting, not yet on fire. This chapter is engagement as invitation — and the name as a check-in, not a nagging soundtrack.

You should have read **Chapter 7** first. If your dog’s body says *not safe*, engagement games wait. Prevention and making it easier beat drilling eye contact out of a frightened dog.

Part IV is where skills meet real life — parks, sofas, doorbells. Engagement and name are the polite knock before we ask for bigger favours. Get them solid in boring rooms first. The exciting world can wait its turn.

**Bookmark this chapter** for name and focus — the base for **come** (Chapter 10) and **walk nicely** (Chapter 11). Basics-first is allowed: a reliable name in the house is a win.

## Section 1 — Engagement games

Engagement is not a staring contest. It is **coming to you pays**. You move away slightly, the dog follows, you mark and pay. You hide behind a door for two seconds, the dog finds you, jackpot. You scatter a treat, they look back at you — mark, pay. Playful, short, optional.

The goal is a dog who thinks checking in with you is a good idea — because history says so — not because you barked their name twelve times outside the supermarket.

Engagement is the opposite of looming. You become interesting by being **predictably fun**, not louder. These games build the same muscle recall will need later — *turning to you is worth it* — without yet asking for a sprint across a field.

### Four starter games

Keep each one to a minute or two. Three to five wins, then stop.

**1. Follow me.** In a quiet room, with treats ready, take two or three steps away from your dog, backwards or sideways. When they follow, mark and treat by your leg. Change direction. Repeat. You are teaching *“when the human moves, staying with them pays.”*

**2. Find it, then find me.** Toss a treat a short way and say “find it”. When the dog eats it and looks back at you, mark and toss another one the other way. A lovely rhythm: away, back, away, back. The look back is the bit you are paying for.

**3. Hide and seek.** With a helper holding the dog gently (or the dog busy with a scatter), step behind a door. Say their name once. When they find you, have a party — several treats, a toy, a cuddle if they like cuddles. Start very easy: half-hidden, one room. This is recall in disguise.

**4. Pay the glance.** No cues at all. Just sit in the garden with a pot of treats. Whenever your dog looks at you on their own, mark and toss a treat. That’s it. Many dogs start “checking in” far more often within a few days.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Play beats drill. If it feels like a job interview, simplify.  
- **Do:** Make checking in profitable.  
- **Do:** Move yourself so following is fun.  
- **Do:** Stop while the dog still wants more.  
- **Don't:** Force eye contact.  
- **Don't:** Corner the dog.  
- **Don't:** Play engagement games while you are furious about the morning walk.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Coming close and looking at you can mean treats, play, or a break from pressure.  
- **Do:** Offer attention to the human when it has paid before.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that humans are boring unless shouted at.

## Section 2 — Name = check-in, not nag

Say the name **once**. One beat. If they turn — eyes, ears, or whole body — mark and pay. If they don’t, the name is not an emergency recall yet. It is a **ping** that should predict good things, not the start of a repeating chorus.

Never use the name to scold. “Rover, Rover, ROVER” for a recall that ends in frustration poisons the word faster than anything in Chapter 5 warned about. Today, the name means *yes, you — good stuff coming*.

Dogs Trust’s guidance treats a good name response as a **foundation for recall and cooperation**, not instant obedience under chaos. That is why it comes here, before Chapter 10.

### Teaching the name game

**You need:** a quiet room, tasty treats, a dog who is awake but not wild.

1. Wait until the dog is near you but not looking at you. Say their name **once**, cheerfully.  
2. The instant they turn their head towards you, **mark**. Treat.  
3. Release, or just let them wander off.  
4. Wait a bit. Repeat. Five or six goes is plenty.  
5. Over a few days, try it when they are a bit further away, or sniffing something mildly interesting.

What counts as success? **Any turn towards you.** Not a sit. Not eye contact held for three seconds. Just the turn. Later you can follow the name with another cue (“Bella — touch”), but the name’s only job is “look this way”.

### Everybody, one name

Dogs end up with a lot of names. Bella, Bells, Bella-boo, Madam, Oi, Trouble. That is fine for chatting. For training, agree with the household that the **proper name, said once, cheerfully** is the one that always pays. Nicknames can stay for cuddles.

And if the name has been poisoned — used mostly for telling off, or shouted endlessly on walks — you are allowed to start fresh. Some owners use a new sound for the training version: a kissy noise, a particular whistle, or a short “pup pup!”. Teach it as above, with lovely pay.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Every repeated name without pay teaches “ignore the first four.”  
- **Do:** Say the name once. Mark any turn towards you. Pay generously.  
- **Don't:** Nag.  
- **Don't:** Pair the name with anger.  
- **Don't:** Test it in chaos before the kitchen is solid.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The name predicts good stuff when taught cleanly.  
- **Do:** Turn when called once. Expect pay for the turn, not a full sit unless that is the game.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that their name means trouble or endless repetition.

## Section 3 — Mild distraction — rules and when to bail

Add **one** mild distraction only when kitchen name games are easy: a toy on the floor, a partner walking past, the door slightly open. Same rule: name once, mark the turn, pay, release. If the turn does not happen, **bail** — do not repeat. Make it easier (more distance, better treat, quieter distraction) or end the session.

This matches later recall work (Chapter 10): the bar rises slowly. **Stop rules** are part of the skill. If the dog stiffens, won’t eat, or backs off, the comfort check failed. Make it easier, prevent, or pause.

Bail rules are a feature, not a failure. **Two misses in a row**, you leave the training picture — easier room, better pay, or done for the day. Dogs learn faster when sessions end before frustration arrives. You are teaching both of you that stopping is allowed.

### A distraction ladder

Here is one way to grow it. Move one line at a time. Each line might take days.

| Step | Where | Distraction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Living room | None |
| 2 | Living room | A toy on the floor, not moving |
| 3 | Kitchen | Someone making a cup of tea |
| 4 | Back garden | Nothing much |
| 5 | Back garden | A toy rolled gently past |
| 6 | Front garden or driveway, on lead | A person walking by on the other side of the road |
| 7 | Quiet park, on a long line | Birds, smells, distant dogs |

You do not need to follow this exactly. The point is: **one small change at a time**, and go back a line if it falls apart.

### Out on walks: pay the check-in

Once your dog knows the name game, take the “pay the glance” game on walks. Leisure walks count. Every time your dog looks back at you, on their own, mark and pay. Not every time forever — but often, at first.

This turns walks into a conversation instead of a tug of war. Many owners find that a dog who checks in a lot is easier to call back, easier to walk on a loose lead, and easier to steer away from trouble. You did not train any of those directly. You just made *paying attention to you* the best deal on the walk.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Distraction level is a dial you control. Not a virtue test.  
- **Do:** Shrink the world when the turn doesn’t come.  
- **Do:** Pay extra-well for the first win in a harder place.  
- **Do:** End early.  
- **Don't:** Stack distractions to impress strangers.  
- **Don't:** Train through worry “for socialisation”.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Success at the current level comes first. Harder places come later.  
- **Do:** Win the turn with mild distractions. Trust that the treat still arrives.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that their name means pressure in scary places.

## Section 4 — “This way!” — a friendly turn

One more small skill, because it saves so many walks. **“This way!”** means *we are turning round together, and it will be fun*.

1. On a quiet walk, with the dog on a loose lead, say “this way!” cheerfully.  
2. Turn and walk briskly in the other direction. Pat your leg if it helps.  
3. When the dog turns and follows, mark and treat by your leg.  
4. Practise lots in easy places, when there is nothing to avoid.

When it is easy, you can use it in real life — the off-lead dog ahead, the worrying bin, the fox smell in the hedge. It is not a punishment or a yank on the lead. It is a cheerful U-turn your dog already knows pays. Chapter 7 explains why distance is so often the answer. This is how you get it smoothly.

## Practice lab — Name game, ten goes max

**You need:** a quiet room, high-value treats, a dog who is not cornered.

**Try this:**

1. Say the name once. Mark any turn towards you. Treat. Release. Reset.  
2. Repeat up to **ten** goes. Stop at **six** if it is still crisp.  
3. If two fail in a row, run the five checks. Better treat, more distance, or stop.  
4. Optional: one go with a mild distraction, but only if the earlier goes were perfect.

**Look for:** a quicker head-turn by the end. A dog who stays near you hopefully.

**If it’s not working:** too many distractions, or the name has bad history. Try a fresh sound in the quietest room.

**Stop if:** the dog moves away, looks worried, or stops taking treats.

A ten-second clip on your phone is optional and slightly undignified. It will show whether you repeated the name. Most of us do, until we train ourselves with the same patience we offer the dog.

## Troubleshooting

- **Dog comes but won’t take the treat** → Stress, or full. Pause. Comfort lens. Do not repeat the name louder.  
- **Only works with food showing** → Keep treats out of sight. Mark the turn, then produce the treat.  
- **Name ignored outside** → Place. More easy goes indoors. Shrink the outdoor world.  
- **Dog turns but looks worried** → Pay and release anyway for the brave turn. Then make it easier — do not hold them in place for more goes.  
- **Dog stares at you all the time and won’t switch off** → Lovely problem, but do give them time off. End sessions clearly with your release and a scatter. Chapter 9 helps with settling.

## Stop if

- Worry, shutdown, or aggression when you approach. Stop. Prevent. See Chapters 7 and 14. Get professional help if needed.  
- You are using the name to scold. Reset the word with easy indoor wins only.  
- The distraction is not mild. Bail to an easier level.
