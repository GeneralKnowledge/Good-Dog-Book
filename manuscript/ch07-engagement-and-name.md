## Opening

Recall, polite walks, and door chaos all lean on the same humble skill: your dog **choosing to orient** to you when the world is merely interesting, not yet on fire. This chapter is engagement as invitation — and the name as a check-in, not a nagging soundtrack.

You should have read **Chapter 12** first: comfort clusters beat single-pixel internet charts. If your dog’s body says *not safe*, engagement games wait. Management and simplify beat drilling eye contact out of a frightened dog.

Part III is where skills meet real life — parks, sofas, doorbells. Engagement and name are the polite knock before we ask for bigger favours. Get them solid in boring rooms first; the exciting world can wait its turn.

## Section 1 — Engagement games

Engagement is not staring contests. It is **approach pays**: you move away slightly, dog follows, mark, pay; you hide behind a door for two seconds, dog finds you, jackpot; you scatter a treat, they look back — mark, pay. Playful, short, opt-in.

The goal is a dog who thinks checking in with you is a good idea — because history says so — not because you barked their name twelve times outside Tesco.

Keep sessions tiny. Three to five wins. Better pay in slightly harder spots later, not heroics today.

Engagement is the opposite of looming: you become interesting by being **predictably fun**, not louder. Move away, let them close the gap, pay. Hide briefly, let them find you, pay bigger. Let them sniff a scatter, then mark the glance back. These games build the same muscle recall will need later — *orienting to you is worth it* — without yet asking for a sprint across a field.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Play beats drill. If it feels like a job interview, simplify.  
- **Do:** Make checking in profitable; move yourself so following is fun; stop while the dog still wants more.  
- **Don't:** Force eye contact; corner; train engagement while you are furious about the morning walk.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Approach and orient can mean treats, play, or relief from social pressure.  
- **Do:** Offer attention to the human when it has paid before.  
- **Don't:** Learn that humans are boring unless shouted at.

## Section 2 — Name = check-in, not nag

Say the name **once**. One beat. If they orient — eyes, ears, or whole body turn — mark and pay. If they do not, the name is not a recall in a crisis yet; it is a **ping** that should predict good things, not a prelude to repetition.

Never use the name to scold. “Rover, Rover, ROVER” for a recall that ends in frustration poisons the word faster than anything in Chapter 5 warned. Name first, then cue later if needed — but today, name = *yes, you; good stuff coming*.

Research-aligned name work treats the response as a **foundation for recall and cooperation**, not instant obedience under chaos (`claim-dt-recall-001-prerequisite-name`, `book-research-name-page-001`).

In busy homes, practise when the dog is already oriented — not as a wake-up shout from another room. Build the association where winning is easy; test it only where you have already banked success. That order is boring and saves you years of shouting.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Every repeated name without pay teaches “ignore the first four.”  
- **Do:** Say name once; mark any orient; pay generously; release.  
- **Don't:** Nag; pair name with anger; test in chaos before the kitchen is solid.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Name predicts good stuff when taught cleanly.  
- **Do:** Turn when called once; expect pay for the orient, not a full sit unless that is the game.  
- **Don't:** Learn that name means trouble or endless repetition.

## Section 3 — Mild distraction — criteria and abort

Add **one** mild distraction only when kitchen name games are easy: toy on floor, partner walking past, door slightly ajar. Same rule: name once, mark orient, pay, release. If orient does not happen, **abort** — do not repeat. Make it easier (distance, reward tier, quieter distraction) or end session.

This aligns with later recall work (Chapter 9): criteria rise slowly; **stop rules** are part of the skill. If the dog stiffens, won't eat, or retreats — comfort check failed. Simplify, manage, or pause. Mild distraction is not “ignore the squirrel”; it is “notice the plastic bag and still choose you sometimes.”

Abort rules are a feature, not a failure: two misses in a row, you **leave** the training picture — easier room, higher pay, or done for the day. Dogs learn faster when sessions end before frustration arrives. You are teaching both of you that stopping is allowed.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Distraction level is a dial you control, not a virtue test.  
- **Do:** Shrink world when orient fails; jackpot the first win in a harder spot; end early.  
- **Don't:** Stack distractions to impress strangers; train through worry because “socialisation.”

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Success at current level first; harder places come later.  
- **Do:** Win orient with mild novelty; trust that pay still arrives.  
- **Don't:** Learn that name means pressure in scary places.

## Practice lab — Name game, ten reps max

Quiet room. High-value treats. Dog not cornered.

1. Say name once. Mark any orient. Pay. Release. Reset.  
2. Repeat up to **ten** reps — stop at **six** if still crisp.  
3. If two fails in a row, run five checks; easier reward or distance; or stop.

Optional: one mild distraction rep only if kitchen reps were flawless.

Film is optional and slightly undignified; a ten-second clip can show whether you repeated the name. Most of us do, until we train ourselves with the same patience we offer the dog.

## Troubleshooting

- **Dog comes but won’t take treat** → Stress or full. Pause; comfort lens; do not repeat name louder.  
- **Only works with food visible** → Fade visible lure; mark orient, then produce treat.  
- **Name ignored outside** → Context. Train more easy reps indoors; shrink outdoor world.  
- **Dog orients but looks worried** → Pay and release anyway for the brave orient, then simplify — do not hold them in place for more reps.

## Stop if

- Worry, shutdown, or aggression around approach — stop; manage; Chapter 12 and 14; professional help if needed.  
- You are using name as scolding — reset the word with easy indoor wins only.  
- Distraction is not mild — abort to easier level.
