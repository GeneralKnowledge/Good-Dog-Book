## Opening

For dogs, the front door often means **excitement**: footsteps, voices, the smell of other people’s coats. Most “bad manners” at the threshold are predictable — and fixable first by **preventing** the chaos (gates, leads, a separate room), not by forcing face-to-face hellos before the dog is ready. If your dog stiffens, barks, or backs away when guests appear, read Chapter 7 first. Comfort beats politeness.

This chapter also covers the other kind of close contact many dogs find hard: **being handled** — paws, ears, mouth, a vet’s hands, a groomer’s comb. The idea is the same. Go slowly, pay generously, and let the dog say “not yet.”

**Bookmark this chapter** for doors, visitors, and vet or grooming prep. If you only want the basics, it can wait until sit, come, and walk nicely are steady.

## Section 1 — Doors: safety before politeness

The door is not a test of who is in charge. It is a **safety pinch point**: darting into traffic, bolting past children, rehearsing excitement every time the bell rings. Teach manners **after** you have prevented disaster.

**Prevention defaults:**

- a baby gate or second barrier between the dog and the front door;  
- the lead on **before** the handle turns, if the dog must be near the door;  
- keys and parcels sorted so you are not juggling while the dog squeezes through;  
- for very excitable dogs, a **separate room** with a stuffed food toy, rather than “sit, stay” in the blast zone while you sign for a parcel.

### Teaching a calm door

This is the same idea as the calm exit in Chapter 11, practised **with nobody there**.

1. Dog behind a gate, or on a lead, before you touch the handle.  
2. Put your hand on the handle. If the dog stays back, mark and treat behind the barrier.  
3. Open the door a crack. Close it. Treat calm.  
4. Open it a bit wider. Close it. Treat.  
5. Step outside and back in. Treat calm.  
6. Release the dog to come through only when you choose — and only if you were going out together anyway.

Over time, the open door stops predicting “escape”. It starts predicting “stay back, get paid.”

### Doorbell barking

Many dogs bark when the doorbell rings or someone knocks. It is natural — they are alerting you. You do not need to stop it completely. You may want it to be shorter.

- **Thank and redirect.** When they bark, calmly say “thank you” and call them away from the door to you or their bed (practise this first with no one there). Pay generously when they come.  
- **Change what the bell means.** Record your doorbell on your phone. Play it very quietly, then drop a treat. Over days, play it a bit louder. Eventually the bell starts to predict treats, not intruders. (Go slowly — if the dog barks, it was too loud.)  
- **Reduce the triggers.** Window film or a closed curtain on the front window. A “no knocking, please leave parcels” note can be a sanity saver while you train.

Shouting “quiet!” usually sounds like joining in.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Every uncontrolled exit teaches “doors explode with freedom.” Your hurry is the enemy.  
- **Do:** Have one routine: dog behind gate or on lead, you open, you release only when they are calm enough.  
- **Do:** Practise with nobody there first.  
- **Don't:** Open the door wide “just once” while the dog rockets past.  
- **Don't:** Shout “sit” from the doorstep after the dog has gone.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Door sounds can predict chaos or calm. Your setup picks which.  
- **Do:** Learn to wait behind a barrier, or go to the mat when the bell rings — starting when excitement is low.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that bolting works because someone always chases.

## Section 2 — Greetings and visitors

The **goal** is calm arrivals — not forced face-to-face hellos. Before any steps, here is the rule that matters most:

> **If the dog is too worried or too wound up, stop.** More distance, the guest steps back or leaves, or you try another day — even if you have not finished the polite script.

What counts as “too much”? Stiffening, staring, whale eye, tight barking, growling, or not being able to take a treat. Any of those means you have gone past what the dog can cope with today (Chapter 7). Stop the approach and go back to prevention.

### The default setup

1. **Lead or secure room before the door opens.** The dog cannot practise jumping, bolting, or snapping at the gap. A baby gate or closed door is fine — preventing chaos is smart.  
2. **Distance matters most.** The guest stays outside or in the hallway. The dog sees or smells them at a distance where their body stays **loose enough to eat**. If they stiffen or bark — the guest steps back, or you move the dog further away.  
3. **One calm helper.** If you practise at all, one adult who follows your rules beats a party of enthusiastic greeters.  
4. **Pay the behaviours you want, at a distance** — quiet four paws, looking at you, lying on the mat — not lunging towards the visitor.

### Scripts for humans (boring on purpose)

Guests are often the hardest part. Give them a script:

- Guest: “I won’t touch until you say. I’ll sit where you point.”  
- You: “If he’s relaxed, you can toss a treat **past** him, not at his face. If not, we’ll just chat.”

No leaning over, no reaching for the head, no “dogs love me” exceptions. Most dogs prefer a visitor who ignores them at first and lets the dog come to them. If the dog chooses to approach, a short stroke on the chest or side is usually better than a pat on the head. Then the guest stops and lets the dog decide whether they want more.

### The knock-to-bed routine

This follows Dogs Trust’s visitor training. It is for dogs who are **excited**, not frightened. Only practise it when the dog is coping comfortably.

1. **Make the knock boring.** Knock quietly on a table, then drop a treat. Build up slowly to louder knocks, then the real door with a helper outside. Pay calm each time.  
2. **Knock means bed.** Teach the dog that a knock is the signal to go to their bed or mat, where they get a long-lasting chew. Practise without anyone coming in.  
3. **Guest comes in while the dog settles.** The helper knocks, the dog goes to bed and starts chewing, the guest walks in and sits down. Nobody fusses the dog.  
4. **Release, then maybe hello.** If the dog is relaxed, give your release word. They can choose to say a calm hello — or not.  
5. **Real visitors.** Use the same routine. If the dog struggles, go back to prevention for that visit.

**Hard stop:** stiffening, growling, or unable to eat means **prevention only** — gate, separate room, no contact with the guest. No flooding to “socialise.”

### Jumping up on visitors

Jumping is usually an excited “hello”. Chapter 2 walked through the fix: prevent jumping from paying (lead, gate), and pay generously for four paws on the floor. Ask guests to turn away calmly if the dog jumps, and to say hello only when the dog’s feet are down. A scatter of treats on the floor as guests arrive keeps noses — and paws — low.

**One idea worth borrowing.** Trainers sometimes teach a **different** behaviour that can’t happen at the same time as the problem — holding a toy at the door instead of jump-barking, or lying on the mat instead of rushing the guest. You are not shouting the problem away. You are paying something else. Popular puppy books including *Easy Peasy Puppy Squeezy* call this idea a **mutually exclusive behaviour**.

### Children and visiting dogs

Children visiting are a special case. Kids move fast, squeal, and reach for faces. Many bites to children happen with a dog they know, at home. Keep it simple:

- **Separate unless supervised.** If you can’t watch every second, the dog goes behind a gate with a chew.  
- **The dog chooses.** Children sit still and let the dog come to them, or not.  
- **No hugs, no kisses, no lying on the dog, no disturbing them in bed.**  
- **Teach children the rule:** “If the dog walks away, we let them go.”

A dog that wants to leave should always be able to.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A polite visitor experience for **humans** may be the wrong goal for **this dog today**. Distance protects everyone’s face.  
- **Do:** Plan greetings like fire drills — where the dog goes, where the guest stands, how you end early.  
- **Don't:** Hold the dog while someone strokes them.  
- **Don't:** Force hand-sniffing rituals.  
- **Don't:** Punish growling and call it training.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Guests can predict pressure or calm. Your setup decides.  
- **Do:** Learn that staying back or looking at you can pay.  
- **Do:** Experience guests as **optional**, not compulsory.  
- **Don't:** Let them practise full-contact greetings while overwhelmed.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that growling is ignored until they have to snap.

## Section 3 — Gentle handling for vet and groom prep

Every dog needs to be handled sometimes: ears checked, nails trimmed, paws cleaned, a tablet given, a vet examination. Handling is **trust-building**, not restraint practice. You are teaching “human hands predict brief, paid touches” — not “you will be grabbed until you give up.”

### The basic method

1. **Pick one body area** — say, a front paw. Start with somewhere the dog doesn’t mind, like the shoulder.  
2. **Touch, treat, take your hand away.** Touch lightly for a second. Mark. Treat. Hand away. The touch predicts the treat.  
3. **Repeat a few times.** Stop while the dog is relaxed.  
4. **Next session, a little more.** Hold the paw for a second. Then lift it slightly. Then touch a nail. Then hold the clippers nearby. One small step at a time, over days or weeks.  
5. **If they pull away, let them.** Do not chase or hold on. Next time, go back to an easier step.

Do the same for ears, mouth (lifting a lip), tail, and gently holding the collar or harness. Two or three minutes a day is plenty.

### Asking the dog: the pause test

A simple way to check whether your dog is enjoying a stroke or a handling session:

1. Stroke or touch for a few seconds.  
2. **Stop and take your hand away.**  
3. Watch. Does the dog lean in, nudge you, or stay close? They are saying “more, please”. Do they move away, shake off, lick their lips, or turn their head? They are saying “that’s enough”.

Listen to the answer. It costs you nothing, and teaches the dog that their signals work — which makes growling and snapping much less likely.

### Vet visits

Your vet’s waiting room is one of the most stressful places many dogs go. You can help:

- **Happy visits.** Ask your practice if you can pop in when it is quiet, step on the scales, get a treat from the reception team, and leave. No needles. No exam. Just a nice time.  
- **Practise at home.** Gentle “mini exams” — looking in ears, lifting lips, touching feet — all paid with treats.  
- **Bring the good stuff.** Jackpot treats, a familiar mat, maybe a favourite toy.  
- **Wait in the car** if the waiting room is busy. Many practices are happy to call you in.  
- **Tell the vet team** if your dog is worried. Many practices take extra care with nervous dogs.

**Muzzle training** is a kindness, not a label. A dog who is calmly used to a basket muzzle — taught slowly with food, like the harness in Chapter 11 — can be examined more safely and with less restraint if they are ever hurt or frightened. If your dog has ever snapped when handled, ask your vet or a qualified professional to help you teach it.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Forced handling teaches fight or freeze. Vet day then costs more.  
- **Do:** Daily three-second touches while the dog is relaxed.  
- **Do:** Use the pause test.  
- **Don't:** Hold the dog down for nail clips while they panic. Do one nail a day if that is what they can cope with.  
- **Don't:** Try to “win” through fear.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Touch can predict treats or predict a trap. Your history decides.  
- **Do:** Learn that holding still for a second earns a treat and an end to the touch.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that growling is punished.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn they must bite before anyone listens.

Growling means **stop and rethink** — slow down, step back, or get professional help. And if handling one particular area is suddenly a problem, see the vet. Pain is a very common reason.

## Practice lab — Pick one

**Comfort first.** If Chapter 7 says this dog is overwhelmed around the door, do **prevention only** — gate, lead, no guest — until their body softens. No practice lab beats a stiff dog.

### Lab A — Door routine with nobody there (five minutes)

**You need:** gate or lead, small treats.

1. Dog **behind the gate or on a lead** before your hand touches the handle.  
2. Open the door a crack. Close it. Pay calm at the barrier. Three goes.  
3. Step outside and back in. Pay before releasing.

**Stop if:** barking spikes, the lead goes tight, or you open “just once” without the setup.

### Lab B — Knock-to-bed (calm dogs only; helper optional)

**You need:** the dog’s bed or mat, a long-lasting chew, a helper.

1. Dog on bed with the chew **before** the knock.  
2. Helper gives **one quiet knock**. You pay calm on the bed. The guest does not come in yet.  
3. Over sessions, build to: knock → guest walks into the hallway while the dog stays on the bed → **release word** before any optional hello.

**Stop if:** stiffening, growling, or the dog won’t eat. Guest leaves. Prevention only next time.

### Lab C — One paw, one week

**You need:** small treats, a relaxed dog, two minutes a day.

1. Day 1–2: touch the paw, treat, hand away. Five goes.  
2. Day 3–4: hold the paw for one second, treat, let go.  
3. Day 5–6: lift the paw slightly, treat. Touch one nail, treat.  
4. Day 7: show the nail clippers, treat. Put them away.

**Stop if:** the dog pulls away more than once, licks their lips, or stiffens. Go back a day.

**Make it easier — then ask again:** a quieter knock, a guest further away, a lighter touch, or Lab A only until the door stops predicting chaos.

## Troubleshooting

- **The dog is fine until the guest moves** → Movement is the trigger. More distance. Ask the guest to stay still. Shorter visits.  
- **The dog is worse on a lead at the door** → They may feel trapped. Try a gate with the dog off lead in a secure room instead.  
- **Guests ignore your rules** → Do not open the door. Prevention includes sending people to the pub.  
- **The dog won’t let you near their feet at all** → Start further away — shoulder, then leg. Use the very best treats. If there is any sign of pain, see the vet.

## Stop if

- Growling, snapping, or trying to bite. **Stop** the approach or the handling. Make the dog safe. See Chapter 14 and get professional help.  
- The dog cannot eat or play at any distance with a guest in view. Too much. The guest leaves. Try another day.  
- You are embarrassed into “just letting them say hi”. **Stop.** Safety beats social niceness.

## Before the next chapter

Life stages come next: puppies, teenagers, and rescues each need a different tempo with the same kind method. Greetings and handling that start slowly in youth save a lot of trouble later.
