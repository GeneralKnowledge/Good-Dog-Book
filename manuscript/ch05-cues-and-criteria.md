## Opening

Your dog can sit for a treat in the kitchen. Congratulations — so can every motivated mammal with knees. The harder art is **what the word means**, **when to ask**, and **when not to** add duration, distance, or the neighbour’s lawnmower as a surprise guest.

This chapter is about clean cues, observable success criteria, and the discipline of raising the bar one rung at a time. It is also about poisoned cues — words that used to mean good things until they meant “ignore me until I shout.”

Criteria are how you stay fair. If you cannot describe what success looks like in one observable sentence, the dog cannot guess your invisible rubric. “Be good” is not a criterion. “Bottom on floor until I count two, then release” is — and even that is only one **D** at a time.

We use words like **cue** and **criterion** here on purpose — plain version: **the word you say** and **what counts as a win**. **Bookmark this chapter** when the dog “knows it at home” but not outside.

## Section 1 — One cue, one meaning

A **cue** is an opportunity to earn pay, not a subpoena. Say it once. If nothing happens, the answer is not volume — it is the five checks from Chapter 4 and **make it easier — then ask again.**

**Poisoned cues** happen when the same word predicts both cheese and chaos — “come” sometimes means treats, sometimes means the end of fun and a cross owner. The dog does not disobey; they **doubt**. One cue, one meaning: if “down” means lie on the floor, it does not also mean “get off the sofa” unless you deliberately teach that chain later.

Fade prompts on purpose: lure becomes smaller, then hand signal, then verbal. If you skip steps, you get a dog who only works when your hand is a ham compass.

Watch for **accidental cues** — the treat bag rustle, your leaning forward, the eyebrow raise that means “I’m about to ask.” Those are not cheating; they are part of the picture. Either fade them on purpose or accept them as context. Surprise comes when you remove them without noticing and then blame the dog for “not listening.”

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Repeating a cue teaches the dog the first three do not count.  
- **Do:**Say the cue once. if no behaviour, simplify and try again — or stop.  
- **Don't:**Nag. change the word mid-stream because you are embarrassed. punish “wrong” guesses when the criterion was unclear.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Cue = predictable chance to win, not a test of loyalty.  
- **Do:** Respond once to a clear signal when the setup is fair.  
- **Don't:** Learn that cues are optional until the human is red-faced.

## Section 2 — Duration, distance, distraction — pick one

The classic three **D**s are where patience goes to die. You want a sit until the kettle boils, at the far end of the garden, while ducks conduct opera. Fine — **not in one session**.

Pick **one** dimension:

- **Duration** — sit stays paid in tiny increments: one second, release, pay; then two.  
- **Distance** — one step away, return, pay; not twenty metres on day one.  
- **Distraction** — one mild novelty: a toy on the floor, not the county show.

New skills deserve **continuous reinforcement** at first: every correct rep pays (`claim-dt-pr-002-continuous-reinforcement-new-skill`). Intermittent pay is a advanced boss level; do not deploy it while the behaviour is still wobbly.

A humble schedule: first teach the behaviour, then steady pay, then — much later — occasional jackpots for excellence in harder places. Owners who “phase out treats” before the behaviour is solid often phase out the behaviour instead. Pay is not a crutch; it is feedback.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Impatience adds three variables and calls it “proofing.”  
- **Do:**Write the one-line criterion. change only one D per session.  
- **Don't:** Stand across the room with ducks and expect a sit you only paid for in silence.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** One dimension at a time keeps the rule readable.  
- **Do:** Succeed at the current level several times before you change it.  
- **Don't:** Learn that “sit” means “maybe, if nothing else is happening.”

## Section 3 — Generalisation without telepathy

Dogs are not bad at generalising — humans are bad at **teaching** generalisation. A behaviour learned in the hallway is a hallway behaviour until you re-teach it elsewhere. New place = new lesson, with the same loop and probably better pay.

**Shrink the world** when context changes: first sit in the new room, then hallway, then garden, then quiet pavement. Each step gets the five-check treatment if things wobble. Proof is a **plan**, not a hope.

Generalisation is also about **you**: different hat, sitting down, holding the lead, talking to a friend. Each variation is a tiny new lesson. Stack them slowly. The dog who “knows sit” at the kitchen table may genuinely not know sit while you are standing by the back door with keys in hand — until you teach that picture on purpose.

### Train yourself

- **Know:**“He knows it at home” is not an insult. it is how learning works.  
- **Do:**Re-run easy reps in each new context. jackpot the first wins in a harder place.  
- **Don't:**Assume telepathy. blame defiance when the criterion was never taught with that distraction present.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** New place, new smells, new rules until proven otherwise.  
- **Do:**Bank paid reps in each environment. grow difficulty slowly.  
- **Don't:**Learn that parks mean failure. learn that cues only work in one room.  

## Practice lab — One generalisation step

Take a behaviour you have looped reliably — sit or hand target. Move to **one** new context: different room, or same room with one mild change (mat on floor, you sitting instead of standing).

- Same one-line criterion.  
- Continuous reinforcement for five to eight reps.  
- Stop while eager.

If the dog cannot succeed, you moved too far. Return to the last winning context; end on a win there.

Write your one-line criterion at the top of a sticky note. When you are tempted to “just see if they can,” read the note. Criteria are how kindness stays concrete instead of vague.

## Troubleshooting

- **Cue stopped working** → Poisoning or nagging. Go back to charged marker + easy reps; one cue only.  
- **Great at home, blank outside** → Context, not attitude. Shrink world; raise pay; shorten session.  
- **You keep adding “stay”** → Release every rep until duration is the **chosen** criterion.

## Stop if

- Stress signs appear in the new place — abort; comfort before criteria.  
- You are comparing to another dog in the same park. Walk away.  
- Session turns into drilling — two good reps and out is allowed here too.
