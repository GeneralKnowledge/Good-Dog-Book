# Chapter 5 — Cues, criteria, and when to raise the bar

## Opening

Your dog can sit for a treat in the kitchen. Congratulations — so can every motivated mammal with knees. The harder art is **what the word means**, **when to ask**, and **when not to** add duration, distance, or the neighbour’s lawnmower as a surprise guest.

This chapter is about clean cues, clear rules for success, and the discipline of raising the bar one rung at a time. It is also about poisoned cues — words that used to mean good things until they meant “ignore me until I shout.” And it teaches the second most-asked-for skill in the book: a calm, happy **down**.

Criteria are how you stay fair. If you cannot describe what success looks like in one plain sentence, the dog cannot guess your invisible marking scheme. “Be good” is not a criterion. “Bottom on floor until I count two, then release” is — and even that is only one thing at a time.

We use words like **cue** and **criterion** here on purpose. Plain version: **the word you say** and **what counts as a win**. **Bookmark this chapter** when the dog “knows it at home” but not outside.

## Section 1 — One cue, one meaning

A **cue** is an opportunity to earn pay, not a summons. Say it once. If nothing happens, the answer is not volume. It is the five checks from Chapter 4, and **make it easier — then ask again.**

**Poisoned cues** happen when the same word predicts both cheese and chaos. “Come” sometimes means treats, sometimes means the end of fun and a cross owner marching home in silence. The dog does not disobey. They **doubt**, the way you doubt a “quick favour” text from someone who still has your ladder.

One cue, one meaning: if “down” means lie on the floor, it does not also mean “get off the sofa”. Use “off” for the sofa. Agree it as a household (the word list in Chapter 3 helps).

Fade prompts on purpose: the lure gets smaller, then becomes a hand signal, then a word. If you skip steps, you get a dog who only works when your hand is a ham compass.

Watch for **accidental cues** — the treat bag rustle, you leaning forward, the eyebrow raise that means “I’m about to ask.” Those are not cheating. They are part of the picture the dog has learned. Either fade them on purpose or accept them as part of the cue. Trouble comes when you remove them without noticing, then blame the dog for “not listening.”

A quick test: stand completely still, hands by your sides, and say the word once. If the dog does it, they know the word. If they stare at your hands, they know the hand signal. Both are fine. Now you know which one you have taught.

### Rescuing a poisoned cue

If a word has gone bad — the dog flinches, freezes, or slopes off when they hear it — do not try to fix it by saying it more. Pick a **new word** and teach it from scratch, with lovely pay and no bad news attached. Many owners do this with recall. “Come” has history. “Here!” or a whistle starts clean.

Then protect the new word. Never use it for something the dog dislikes. If you must do something unpleasant — nail trim, bath, end of the park — go and get the dog quietly instead of calling.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Repeating a cue teaches the dog the first three do not count.  
- **Do:** Say the cue once.  
- **Do:** If nothing happens, make it easier and try again — or stop.  
- **Don't:** Nag.  
- **Don't:** Change the word halfway through because you are embarrassed.  
- **Don't:** Punish “wrong” guesses when your rule was unclear.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** A cue is a predictable chance to win. Not a test of loyalty.  
- **Do:** Respond once to a clear signal when the setup is fair.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that cues are optional until the human is red-faced.

## Section 2 — Teaching down

“Down” — lying on the floor — is one of the most useful things a dog can know. A dog lying down cannot jump on the visitor, pull towards the café table, or bounce off the walls. It is the starting point for settling (Chapter 9) and a calm way to wait anywhere.

It is also a behaviour many dogs find a little harder than sit, because lying down puts them in a more vulnerable position. A dog who is worried, cold, or on a hard, slippery floor may not want to lie down — and that is fair. Start somewhere warm, soft, and quiet. A rug or a mat is ideal.

**You need:** a soft surface, small treats, your marker and release words. Your dog should already know sit (Chapter 4).

### Step 1 — Follow the treat to the floor (no word yet)

1. Ask for a sit. Mark and treat it.  
2. Hold a new treat at the dog’s nose.  
3. Move your hand slowly **straight down** towards the floor, just in front of their front paws. Think “nose to chest, then chest to floor.”  
4. Many dogs follow the treat down and fold into a lying position. The moment their elbows and chest touch the floor, **mark**. Give the treat while they are still lying down.  
5. Release with your free word. Toss a treat so they get up.  
6. Five goes. Stop.

### If the dog will not go all the way down

Some dogs stretch their neck down and leave their bottom in the air like a bridge. That is fine. **Pay for smaller steps** first:

- head dipping towards the floor — mark and treat;  
- then elbows bending a little — mark and treat;  
- then elbows bending a lot — mark and treat;  
- then chest on the floor.

This is shaping, from Chapter 4. Each session, ask for just a little more. If you get stuck, try sitting on the floor yourself with your knee raised, and lure the dog under the tunnel of your leg. Many dogs lie down to get through.

You can also **capture** it. Keep a pot of treats in the living room. Every time the dog lies down on their own, calmly mark and place a treat between their paws. Some dogs learn down faster this way than any other.

**Never push the dog’s back or pull their legs to get them down.** It is unpleasant, it makes lying down feel unsafe, and it teaches nothing except to avoid your hands. Show them what you want and pay for it.

### Step 2 — Empty hand

1. Make the same downward hand movement, with **no treat in it**.  
2. When they lie down, mark, then give a treat from your pouch or other hand.  
3. Repeat over a few sessions until the empty hand works smoothly.

### Step 3 — Add the word

1. Once the dog lies down easily for the hand signal, say “down” once, in a normal voice.  
2. Pause for a breath. Then give the hand signal.  
3. Mark the down. Treat while they are lying there. Release.  
4. Over time, make the hand signal smaller and later, until the word does the work.

### Step 4 — A little longer

Once down is easy, you can start to build time. While the dog is lying down, give a few treats in a row, slowly, placed between their paws. Then release. This teaches that lying down is where good things keep happening. Next section explains how to add time without breaking it.

### Step 5 — Other places

Practise in another room. Then the garden. Then, much later, somewhere quieter outside, on a lead. Each new place is a new lesson, so start with a tiny lure again if you need to. A soft mat that travels with you helps a lot.

**Look for:** a dog who folds down easily, with soft body and relaxed face, and who looks a bit pleased about it.

**If it’s not working:** check the floor (soft?), the place (quiet?), and the dog’s comfort. Some dogs with sore joints, deep chests, or short coats really do not want to lie on a cold, hard floor. If lying down seems uncomfortable, or the dog used to do it and has stopped, ask your vet.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Down can feel vulnerable to a dog. Patience here pays back for years.  
- **Do:** Start on a soft surface in a quiet room.  
- **Do:** Pay for small steps if the full down will not come.  
- **Don't:** Push, pull, or lean over the dog.  
- **Don't:** Use “down” to mean “get off” as well.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Lying down is just a movement that pays, then a comfy place to wait.  
- **Do:** Learn the hand signal first, then the word.  
- **Don't:** Rush to duration before the down itself is easy.

## Section 3 — Duration, distance, distraction — pick one

The classic three **D**s — **duration, distance, distraction** — are where patience goes to die. You will see them in many kind training books (Steve Mann’s *Easy Peasy Puppy Squeezy* is one famous UK example). Same rule here: **one D per session**, not all three because you are bored on a Sunday.

You want a down until the kettle boils, at the far end of the garden, while ducks perform opera. Fine — **not in one session**.

Pick **one** dimension:

- **Duration** — the down lasts a little longer: one second, release, pay. Then two.  
- **Distance** — you take one step away, come back, pay. Not twenty metres on day one.  
- **Distraction** — one mild extra: a toy on the floor, not the county show.

When you make one D harder, make the others **easier**. Taking a step back for the first time? Ask for a very short down. Adding a toy on the floor? Stand right next to the dog.

### A duration ladder

Here is how building time actually looks. Each line is a session or two, not a minute:

| Step | You ask for | Then |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Down, treat, release | Easy wins |
| 2 | Down, count “one”, treat, release | Stay close |
| 3 | Down, count “one, two, three”, treat, release | Mix in easy ones |
| 4 | Down, two treats a few seconds apart, release | Calm treats between paws |
| 5 | Down for about ten seconds, three treats spread out, release | Mix in short ones |
| 6 | Down for thirty seconds while you sit on the sofa | Still pay several times |

Notice two things. First, you **mix in easy ones**. If every go is harder than the last, training becomes a test the dog keeps losing. Second, you **release before they break**. If the dog gets up before you said “free”, you asked for a bit too long. Next go, ask for less.

New skills deserve **steady pay** at first: every correct go earns a treat. Paying only sometimes is an advanced level. Do not try it while the behaviour is still wobbly — or while you are still finding the treat bag with your eyes closed.

A humble plan: first teach the behaviour, then pay every time, then — much later — mix in occasional jackpots for excellence in harder places. Owners who “phase out treats” before the behaviour is solid often phase out the behaviour instead. Pay is not a crutch. It is feedback.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Impatience adds three variables and calls it “progress.”  
- **Do:** Write your one-line rule.  
- **Do:** Change only one D per session, and make the others easier.  
- **Don't:** Stand across the room with ducks and expect a down you only paid for in silence.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** One dimension at a time keeps the rule readable.  
- **Do:** Succeed at the current level several times before it changes.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that “down” means “maybe, if nothing else is happening.”

## Section 4 — Generalisation without telepathy

Dogs are not bad at generalising. Humans are bad at **teaching** it. A behaviour learned in the hallway is a hallway behaviour until you re-teach it elsewhere. New place, new lesson — same loop, probably better pay.

**Shrink the world** when the place changes: first the new room, then the hallway, then the garden, then a quiet pavement. Each step gets the five checks if things wobble. Reliability is a **plan**, not a hope.

Generalisation is also about **you**: different hat, sitting down, holding the lead, talking to a friend. Each variation is a tiny new lesson. Stack them slowly. The dog who “knows sit” at the kitchen table may genuinely not know sit while you are standing by the back door with keys in hand — until you teach that picture on purpose.

A simple way to plan: write down five places, from easiest to hardest. Something like:

1. Living room  
2. Kitchen  
3. Back garden  
4. Front garden or driveway  
5. Quiet corner of the park, on a lead

Move one line at a time. If it falls apart, go back one line, win twice, and finish. You will often find the dog jumps a few lines quickly once they have the idea.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “He knows it at home” is not an insult. It is how learning works.  
- **Do:** Re-run easy goes in each new place.  
- **Do:** Pay extra-well for the first wins somewhere harder.  
- **Don't:** Assume telepathy.  
- **Don't:** Blame defiance when the skill was never taught with that distraction around.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** New place, new smells, new rules — until proven otherwise.  
- **Do:** Bank paid goes in each place. Grow difficulty slowly.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that parks mean failure.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that cues only work in one room.

## Practice lab — One step harder

Take a behaviour you can do reliably — sit, down, or the hand touch. Change **one** thing: a different room, *or* one more second of duration, *or* one step of distance, *or* one mild distraction.

**You need:** a sticky note, a pen, small treats.

**Try this:**

1. Write your one-line rule on the sticky note. Example: *“Down in the kitchen, three seconds, I stay next to her.”*  
2. Pay every correct go. Five to eight goes.  
3. Stop while the dog is keen.

**Look for:** at least four or five clean wins. Then you can move on next time.

**If it’s not working:** you moved too far. Go back to the last thing that worked. End on a win there.

**Stop if:** the dog looks worried in the new place, or you hear yourself say “come *on*”.

When you are tempted to “just see if they can,” read the sticky note. Rules are how kindness stays concrete instead of vague.

## Troubleshooting

- **Cue stopped working** → Poisoned or nagged. Go back to easy goes, one cue only. Or teach a fresh word.  
- **Great at home, blank outside** → The place, not attitude. Shrink the world. Raise the pay. Shorten the session.  
- **Dog pops up out of the down** → You asked for too long. Release sooner. Deliver treats low, between the paws.  
- **Dog creeps towards you in the down** → Deliver treats to them, not by your feet. Step back towards them to pay.  
- **You keep adding “stay”** → Release every go until duration is the rule you **chose** for today.

## Stop if

- Stress signs appear in the new place. Stop. Comfort before criteria.  
- You are comparing to another dog in the same park. Walk away.  
- The session turns into drilling. Two good goes and out is allowed here too.
