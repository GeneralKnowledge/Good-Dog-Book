## Opening

You have the deal, the learning theory, and a treat bag that does not rattle like a maraca. Now the routine — the bit you will repeat for sits, downs, settles, recalls, and “please do not mug the postman.”

The **teaching loop** is deliberately boring: **setup → cue → behaviour → mark and reward → release → reset.** Boring is good. Boring means you are not improvising a new religion every evening — or narrating each go like a sports commentator because the neighbour can see over the fence. This chapter installs the loop on one simple behaviour — sit, or the hand touch from Chapter 3 — and teaches you what to do when nothing happens except your own rising blood pressure.

Chapter 1 said it in plain words: **get ready → ask once → pay → let them off → start fresh.** From here we use a few trainer words on purpose. Same kindness, clearer steps. **Bookmark Chapter 4** when you want the full routine for any skill.

## Section 1 — The loop in prose

Walk through it once, slowly:

1. **Setup** — Dog able to focus. Rewards ready. Room dull enough to win.  
2. **Cue** — One clear signal, said **once**.  
3. **Behaviour** — The dog does the thing (or the small slice of it you are paying for today).  
4. **Mark and reward** — Marker on the behaviour. Treat or play follows.  
5. **Release** — “Free”, or a step away. It means *that go is done*. You are not holding them in limbo.  
6. **Reset** — Breathe, reposition, neutral hands. The next go is a fresh go.

The dog is learning: *that sound predicts pay; that movement is worth repeating; the release is information too.* You are learning: *one cue, fair pay, stop while it is still fun.*

The release matters more than owners think. Without it, “sit” becomes an indefinite freeze while you fish for treats. A clear break — cheerful, consistent — tells the dog the go ended successfully. Then reset: neutral posture, no looming, no “why aren’t you sitting again yet.”

### What a “cue” actually is

A cue is any signal that tells the dog *this behaviour will pay now*. It can be a word (“sit”), a hand signal, or something in the world (the doorbell, the lead coming off the hook). It is an **invitation**, not a command with a threat behind it.

Here is the part that surprises people: **you often add the word last**, not first. While the dog is still working out the movement, the word is just noise. Once they are doing the movement easily, you start saying the word just before, so it comes to predict the movement. Say “sit” forty times to a dog who does not know sit, and you mostly teach them that “sit” is a sound humans make.

### Three ways to get the behaviour

You cannot pay for something that never happens. There are three kind ways to get a behaviour started:

- **Luring** — a treat at the nose guides the dog into position. Fast and clear. The catch: you must fade the treat out, or you have a dog who only sits when your hand smells of ham.  
- **Capturing** — wait for the dog to do it naturally, then mark and pay. Brilliant for lying down, settling, and looking at you. Slower to start, but the dog often learns it deeply.  
- **Shaping** — pay for small steps towards the final behaviour. Head dips, then elbows bend, then chest touches the floor. Very useful when luring does not work.

All three use the same loop. Pick whichever gets you a clean win soonest. There is no prize for doing it the hard way.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Timing beats charisma. Late marks pay for the wrong picture.  
- **Do:** Cue once.  
- **Do:** Mark while the behaviour is happening.  
- **Do:** Release every go, so “stay” is not smuggled in by accident.  
- **Don't:** Stack five cues.  
- **Don't:** Keep going past the wag.  
- **Don't:** Train while you are already late for something.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Behaviour, then marker, then pay. That is the association.  
- **Know:** The release ends the go.  
- **Do:** Earn the mark with today’s agreed movement — a full sit, or an honest nose touch to the palm.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that only the third “sit sit SIT” counts.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that training never ends.

## Section 2 — Teaching sit, start to finish

Here is the whole loop on one behaviour. It takes most dogs a few short sessions over a few days. Do one step per session if that is what the dog needs.

**You need:** a quiet room, a non-slip floor, small soft treats, your marker word, your release word.

**Step 1 — Lure the movement (no word yet)**

1. Hold a treat at the dog’s nose. Let them sniff it.  
2. Slowly lift the treat up and slightly back over their head. As the nose goes up, the bottom tends to go down.  
3. The instant the bottom touches the floor, **mark**. Give the treat.  
4. Release with your free word. Toss a treat a step away so they stand up and come back.  
5. Repeat five times. Stop.

If the dog jumps up, your hand is too high. Keep it just above the nose. If they back up, try with a wall behind them, or on a rug.

**Step 2 — Same movement, empty hand**

1. Make the same hand movement with **no treat in it**. Keep the treats in a pouch or your other hand.  
2. Bottom touches the floor: mark, then treat from the pouch.  
3. Five goes. Stop.

This is the moment many owners skip. It is the moment the dog learns that the hand is a signal, not a sausage dispenser.

**Step 3 — Add the word**

1. Say “sit” once, in a normal voice.  
2. Pause for a breath. Then give the hand signal.  
3. Mark the sit. Treat. Release.  
4. Over a few sessions, make the hand signal smaller and later. Eventually the dog sits on the word alone.

**Step 4 — Easy variations**

Ask for sit with you standing, then sitting on a chair, then in another room. One change at a time. Chapter 5 explains why.

**Look for:** a dog who sits quickly and looks pleased with themselves. That is a learned behaviour.

**If it’s not working:** go back a step. Most stuck sits are a dog who was moved on to the word before the hand signal was easy.

## Section 3 — Splitting the ask — one thing per session

**Criteria** are what you are paying for this session — not the whole finished trick from television. One session might pay for *bottom touches floor for one second*. Another pays for *nose to palm without teeth*. If you pay for duration, distance, and distraction all at once, you are not raising the bar. You are building a trapdoor.

**Lure, then fade:** the lure teaches the motion. The hand signal comes when the motion is reliable. The word comes when the hand signal is reliable. Rush any step and you get a dog who only works when your hand smells like ham.

Write a one-line success rule before you start: *“I mark when elbows hit the ground”* or *“I mark when nose touches palm, no teeth.”* If you cannot say it in one line, you are asking for too much at once.

A good rule of thumb: if the dog gets it right about **eight times out of ten**, it is time to make it a tiny bit harder. If they get it right less than half the time, make it easier. In between, keep going as you are.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** One rule per session is a kindness to future you.  
- **Do:** Decide what earns the mark **before** you start.  
- **Do:** Celebrate honest slices of the behaviour.  
- **Don't:** Add duration because you are bored.  
- **Don't:** Move the goalposts mid-go because you saw a better dog online.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** What earns the mark **today** is the lesson — not yesterday’s version in a harder room.  
- **Do:** Repeat the paid version until it is easy. Then change **one** thing next time.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn muddy rules, where half-sits sometimes pay and sometimes do not.

## Section 4 — When nothing happens — five checks and the mantra

Silence is data. Before you repeat the cue like a broken car alarm, run the **five checks**:

1. **Setup** — Quiet enough? Can the dog physically succeed?  
2. **Reward** — Is the pay worth showing up for **right now**?  
3. **Ask** — One clear ask, or three hidden ones?  
4. **Comfort** — Any cluster of stress signs? (Chapter 7 goes deeper.)  
5. **Place** — Did it work indoors but fail outdoors? **Shrink the world** until success is likely again.

Then say the owner mantra — out loud if it helps:

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

Easier might mean lure again, a smaller slice, a better treat, a closer distance, fewer witnesses. It is not defeat. It is engineering. Optional habit: when you fix the plan instead of blaming the dog, murmur **“Good human.”** — a marker for the trainer in the room.

Louder is not a teaching strategy. Neither is the sigh that makes the dog shrink. Make it easier, then ask **once**.

### A stalled sit, walked through

You are in the kitchen. You say “sit”. The dog looks at you, looks at the fridge, and lies down on the tiles like a seal. You say “sit” again. Nothing. You feel the heat start.

- **Setup:** the tiles are slippery. Move to a rug.  
- **Reward:** kibble, near the fridge, where the good stuff lives. Swap to chicken.  
- **Ask:** you were standing up, holding a mug. You trained sitting on the sofa. That is a new picture. Put the mug down.  
- **Comfort:** ears soft, tail loose, keen to eat. Fine.  
- **Place:** the kitchen at teatime is a busier room than the lounge at 10 a.m.

Make it easier: rug, chicken, empty hands, a tiny lure. Bottom hits the floor. Mark. Treat. Free. End. *Good human.*

When the mantra works, you will feel almost let down — no drama, just a smaller ask and a paid go. That anticlimax is the sound of trust being rebuilt. Chapter 14 prints the full five-check table for the fridge door.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Frustration is information about the plan. Usually the ask or the place. Not “disrespect.”  
- **Do:** Run the five checks.  
- **Do:** Change setup or reward before you change volume.  
- **Do:** End the session if you are heating up.  
- **Don't:** Repeat cues.  
- **Don't:** Corner the dog.  
- **Don't:** Train through shutdown because the neighbour is watching.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Easier goes rebuild confidence. Failed goes in chaos teach hesitation.  
- **Do:** Get paid for a smaller success, then grow.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that cues are noise.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that humans get louder when confused.

## Section 5 — Keep a tiny record

You do not need a spreadsheet. You need one line after each session, because memory is a liar. Without notes, every session feels like “he’s still not getting it”. With notes, you see that Tuesday was three out of five and Saturday was five out of five.

Try this format, in a notebook or your phone:

> *Date — skill — where — reward — how many right out of how many — one thing to change next time.*
>
> *Mon — sit, word only — kitchen — cheese — 4/5 — try in hallway.*  
> *Wed — sit — hallway — cheese — 2/5 — back to kitchen; dog tired after walk.*

That last note is the gold. It tells you *why*, so next time you train before the walk instead of after it.

## Practice lab — Full loop, stop while eager

**You need:** a charged marker, ranked treats, a boring room.

**Try this:**

1. Pick **sit** or the **hand touch**. Write your one-line rule.  
2. One cue per go. Mark. Treat. Release. Reset.  
3. Five to eight goes maximum.  
4. Stop after several clean wins, **before** the dog wanders off or you get sloppy.  
5. Write your one-line record.

**Look for:** a quicker response by the last few goes. A dog who stays near you when you stop.

**If it’s not working:** if two goes fail in a row, run the five checks, make it easier, try once. If still stuck, end the session. Tomorrow is a gift, and the dog will not file a complaint with Ofsted.

**Stop if:** voices rise, the dog stiffens or wanders off, or you catch yourself counting reps through gritted teeth.

## Troubleshooting

- **Dog jumps for the treat** → Mark only four paws down, or the nose touch. Do not pay the leap. Keep the lure lower. Reset without scolding.  
- **“He only does it with food in my hand.”** → Expected early on. Go back to Step 2 — empty hand, treat from the pouch.  
- **Dog sits, then pops straight up** → Mark faster, while the bottom is still on the floor. Then deliver the treat low, so they stay down to eat it.  
- **You forgot the release** → The dog learns that holding still is infinite — or that they decide when it ends. Release every go.  
- **Dog offers everything except what you asked** → Lovely. They are trying. Make the next go easier and pay quickly. Do not punish the guessing.

## Stop if

- Voices rise, the dog stiffens, you grip treats like weapons. **Stop.**  
- The comfort check fails. See Chapter 7. Prevent or pause.  
- You are training to vent. Walk away.
