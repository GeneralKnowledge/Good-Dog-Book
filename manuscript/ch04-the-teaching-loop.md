## Opening

You have the deal, the learning theory, and a treat bag that does not rattle like a maraca. Now the choreography — the bit you will repeat for sits, settles, recalls, and “please do not mug the postman.”

The **teaching loop** is deliberately boring: **setup → cue → behaviour → mark/reward → release → reset.** Boring is good. Boring means you are not improvising a new religion every evening — or narrating each rep like a sports commentator because the neighbour can see through the fence. This chapter installs the loop on one simple behaviour — sit or hand target — and teaches you what to do when nothing happens except your own rising blood pressure.

Chapter 1 said it in plain words: **get ready → ask once → pay → let them off → start fresh.** From here we use the trainer labels on purpose. Same kindness, clearer steps. **Bookmark Chapter 4** when you want the full habit for any skill.

## Section 1 — The loop in prose

Walk through it once, slowly:

1. **Setup** — Dog able to focus; rewards ready; environment dull enough to win.  
2. **Cue** — One clear signal, said **once**.  
3. **Behaviour** — The dog does the thing (or a slice of it you are paying today).  
4. **Mark/reward** — Marker on the behaviour; treat or play follows.  
5. **Release** — “Free,” “ok,” or a step away — means *that rep is done*; you are not holding them in limbo.  
6. **Reset** — Breathe, reposition, neutral hands. Next rep is a fresh rep.

The dog is learning: *that sound predicts pay; that movement is worth repeating; release is information too.* You are learning: *one cue, fair pay, stop while it is still fun.*

For a new sit, a lure can start the movement — food at the nose, lift slightly so the bottom finds the floor — then mark the sit, pay, release. Fade the lure later (Chapter 5); today, clarity beats vanity.

**Hand target** follows the same loop: present palm, dog touches nose, mark, pay, release. It is a brilliant first behaviour because it is easy to see, easy to pay, and builds “orient to my hand” for later skills. Either choice is fine; pick one for this chapter and finish the loop before you collect the other.

The release matters more than owners think. Without it, “sit” becomes an indefinite freeze while you fish for treats. A clear break — cheerful, consistent — tells the dog the rep ended successfully. Then reset: neutral posture, no looming, no “why aren’t you sitting again yet.”

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Timing beats charisma. Late marks pay for the wrong picture.  
- **Do:** Cue once. mark while the behaviour is happening. release every rep so “stay” is not smuggled in by accident.  
- **Don't:** Stack five cues. drill past the wag. train while you are already late for something.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Behaviour → marker → pay is the association. release ends the trial.  
- **Do:** Earn the mark with the agreed movement today — full sit or honest hand touch to palm.  
- **Don't:** Learn that only the third “sit sit SIT” counts. learn that training never ends.  

## Section 2 — Splitting criteria — one thing per session

**Criteria** are what you are paying for this session — not the whole finished trick on television. One session might pay for *bottom touches floor for one second*. Another pays for *nose to palm without biting*. If you pay for duration, distance, and distraction simultaneously, you are not raising the bar — you are building a trapdoor.

**Lure → fade** preview: the lure teaches the motion; the cue comes when the motion is reliable; the lure shrinks to a hand signal, then a verbal cue alone. Rush the fade and you get a dog who only works when your hand smells like ham.

Write a one-line success criterion before you start: *“I mark when elbows hit the ground”* or *“I mark when nose touches palm, no teeth.”* If you cannot say it in one line, you have too many criteria.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** One criterion per session is a kindness to future you.  
- **Do:** Decide what earns the mark **before** you cue. celebrate honest slices of the behaviour.  
- **Don't:** Add duration because you are bored. move the goalposts mid-rep because Instagram exists.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** What earns the mark **today** is the lesson — not yesterday’s version in a harder room.  
- **Do:** Repeat the paid version until easy, then change **one** thing next time.  
- **Don't:** Learn muddy rules where sometimes half-sits pay and sometimes they do not.

## Section 3 — When nothing happens — five checks and the mantra

Silence is data. Before you repeat the cue like a broken car alarm, run the **five checks**:

1. **Setup** — Quiet enough? Can the dog physically succeed?  
2. **Reward** — Is the pay worth showing up for **right now**?  
3. **Criterion** — One clear ask, or three hidden ones?  
4. **Comfort** — Any stress cluster? (Chapter 12 deepens this.)  
5. **Context** — Did it work indoors but fail outdoors? **Shrink the world** until success is likely again.

Then say the owner mantra — out loud if it helps:

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

Easier might mean lure again, smaller slice, better treat, closer distance, fewer witnesses. It is not defeat. It is engineering. Optional habit: when you fix the plan instead of blaming the dog, murmur **“Good human.”** — a marker for the trainer in the room (`book-claim-owner-mantra`).

Louder is not a teaching strategy. Neither is the sigh that makes the dog shrink. Make it easier, then ask **once**.

Walk through a stalled sit as an example. **Setup:** move off the slippery floor to a rug. **Reward:** swap kibble for chicken. **Criterion:** pay for elbows bending, not a perfect show sit. **Comfort:** ears forward, willing to eat? If not, stop — comfort beats criteria. **Context:** were you asking in the garden when you only trained indoors? Shrink to the hallway, win once, end. That sequence is the book’s troubleshooting spine; Chapter 14 prints the full table.

When the mantra works, you will feel almost anticlimactic — no drama, just a smaller ask and a paid rep. That anticlimax is the sound of trust being rebuilt.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Frustration is information about the plan — usually criterion or context, not “disrespect.”  
- **Do:** Run the five checks. change setup or reward before you change volume. end session if you are heating up.  
- **Don't:** Repeat cues. corner the dog. train through shutdown because you are embarrassed in front of the neighbour.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Easier reps rebuild confidence. failed reps in chaos teach hesitation.  
- **Do:** Get paid for a smaller success, then grow.  
- **Don't:** Learn that cues are noise. learn that humans escalate when confused.  

## Practice lab — Full loop, stop while eager

Pick **sit** or **hand target**. Five to eight reps maximum.

- Setup: charged marker, ranked reward, boring room.  
- One cue per rep; mark; pay; release; reset.  
- Stop after several clean wins **before** the dog wanders or you get sloppy.

If two reps fail in a row, run the five checks, make it easier, try once. If still stuck, end session — tomorrow is a gift, and the dog will not file a complaint with Ofsted.

## Troubleshooting

- **Dog jumps for the treat** → Mark only four paws down or the target touch; do not pay the leap. Reset without scolding.  
- **“He only does it with food in my hand.”** → Expected early on. Fade lure in Chapter 5; today, clarity first.  
- **You forgot the release** → Dog learns holding still is infinite. Break every rep.

## Stop if

- Voices rise, dog stiffens, you grip treats like weapons. **Stop.**  
- Comfort check fails — see Chapter 12. Manage or pause.  
- You are training to vent. Walk away.
