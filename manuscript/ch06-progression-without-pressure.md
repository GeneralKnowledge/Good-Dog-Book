## Opening

You have a loop, cues that mean something, and a dog who sometimes cooperates like a dream and sometimes like a teenager with headphones. Progress in real kitchens is not a straight line. It is a scribble with good days, weird days, and the occasional “what happened to the sit we had on Tuesday?”

This chapter is about progress from the owner’s side: when to practise what you have instead of chasing new things, how to read setbacks without getting cross, and when **simplifying** is success and **getting help** is wisdom — not defeat. Nobody gives you a rosette for the fifth “new trick” your dog performs once on camera and never again.

If Chapter 4 gave you the loop and Chapter 5 gave you clear rules, Chapter 6 gives you **permission** — to repeat, to pause, to call the vet, to hire help. Progress without pressure is not laziness. It is how skilled trainers avoid burning trust on a Tuesday.

Most owners only need **sit, down, come, and walk nicely**. Bank practice at the current level instead of chasing new tricks. **Bookmark this chapter** when progress wobbles but nobody is unsafe.

## Section 1 — Practice beats novelty

Fluency is boring. It is also the point. Five clean sits in the hallway with one cue and happy pay beat a dozen new tricks performed once on video. **Consolidation** — paid practice at the current level until it is easy — is how behaviours survive bad moods and busy parks.

Boredom in the human is not a signal to add three Ds and a circus. It is often a signal to try the skill in **one** new place, play an engagement game (Chapter 8), or end the session and live your life. Dogs do not need a new command every week. They need reliable basics that still work when you are tired.

Fluency also protects **you** on bad days. When the sit is easy, you need less brain space during the doorbell crisis. That is the payoff for repetitive, unglamorous practice — not a ribbon, but headroom.

### Making practice fun (for both of you)

Practice does not have to mean the same five sits in the same spot. Mix it up without making it harder:

- **Mix and match.** Ask for sit, then a hand touch, then down, then free. Pay each one. Dogs often enjoy the variety.  
- **Find it.** After a good go, toss the treat and say “find it”. A quick sniff-and-search is a reward and a reset in one.  
- **Life rewards.** Ask for a sit before the door opens for the garden. The door opening is the pay. Ask for a down before the dinner bowl goes on the floor.  
- **Thirty-second bursts.** Three easy cues while the kettle boils. Done. These add up more than one heroic weekend session.

A good test of fluency: can the dog do it the **first** time you ask, in this place, with no lure? If so, it is ready to try somewhere a bit harder. If not, more practice here.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Novelty is shiny. Fluency is what you need when the doorbell rings.  
- **Do:** Bank practice at the current level.  
- **Do:** Celebrate “boring” success.  
- **Don't:** Make it harder because you are restless.  
- **Don't:** Call the dog “stubborn” when you skipped the practice.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Repetition with pay makes behaviour sturdy.  
- **Do:** Repeat what works at this level until it is easy. Then change one thing.  
- **Don't:** Leave them with a graveyard of half-taught tricks.  
- **Don't:** Make every session harder than the last.

## Section 2 — Setbacks: the usual suspects

Setbacks have causes. Before you decide the dog has “forgotten” or is “testing you”, run through the usual suspects:

| Suspect | Looks like | Try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The place changed** | Fine at home, falls apart at the park | Go back to the last place that worked. Move up more slowly. |
| **Life changed** | New baby, builders, house move, new pet, family member away | Lower expectations for a few weeks. More prevention, more rest. |
| **Health** | Sudden change, stiffness, licking, not eating, more sensitive to touch | **Vet first.** Pause training. |
| **Sleep** | Silly, mouthy, can’t focus, especially in the evening | More naps. Shorter sessions. Train earlier in the day. |
| **Age** | A teenager who suddenly finds everything more interesting than you | Same method, smaller world, more patience. See Chapter 13. |
| **The old habit is paying again** | Jumping, barking, or pulling creeping back | Find out what is rewarding it. Block that. Pay the alternative. |

That last row deserves a closer look. Often, nothing is wrong with the training. Something in daily life has started **paying** for the old behaviour again — a visitor who loves being jumped on, a family member who opens the door when the dog barks, a neighbour feeding scraps over the fence. The dog is not being difficult. The dog is being consistent with the deal they are actually getting. Track down the leak and plug it.

**Health** gets its own warning. Pain and illness make everything harder, and they often look like “bad behaviour” — grumpiness, not wanting to sit, snapping when touched, toilet accidents, a dog who suddenly does not want to walk. If a change is sudden, or you have a nagging feeling, **see the vet before you train harder.** Training through pain is unkind and does not work.

### Adolescence: a note of mercy

The dog who nailed recall at five months may audition for independence at twelve. Their brain and body are changing. Priorities shift. Yesterday’s star pupil discovers squirrels have opinions.

Same method, slower tempo, more prevention — gates, shorter walks, a long line — and fewer street sermons delivered to a squirrel. You are not starting from zero. You are re-banking trust while the brain remodels. Owners who read this stage as defiance often get stricter and louder. Owners who shrink the world usually come out the other side with the relationship intact. Chapter 13 has more.

### A bad-week plan

Sometimes it is not the dog. It is you. Illness, work, grief, a new baby, simply too much. On those weeks:

1. **Stop teaching new things.** Completely. It is fine.  
2. **Prevent the big problems.** Gates up. Lead on near the door. Walks somewhere quiet.  
3. **Keep two easy habits ticking over.** Maybe a sit before dinner and a scatter of treats in the garden.  
4. **Rest.** Chews, sniff walks, naps for everybody.  
5. **Pick it back up** when you have the headroom. Start one level easier than where you left off.

This is not falling behind. It is not damaging what you built. Dogs cope remarkably well with a quiet week. They cope badly with an owner who is trying to train through exhaustion.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Setbacks are usually place, life, health, sleep, age, or an old habit paying again.  
- **Know:** They are rarely betrayal.  
- **Do:** Check health when a change is sudden.  
- **Do:** Lower the bar. Prevent what you cannot teach today.  
- **Don't:** Train through pain.  
- **Don't:** Punish going backwards.  
- **Don't:** Meet teenage energy with your worst Tuesday.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** When the body or the world is wrong, “try harder” is nonsense.  
- **Do:** Succeed small again. Trust the loop in quieter conditions.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that humans get scary when life is hard.

## Section 3 — When to simplify vs when to get help

**Simplify** when: the setup was too hard, the reward too weak, the ask muddy, the dog uncomfortable, or the place jumped too far. That covers most bad sessions. Fix the plan, not the dog. **Make it easier — then ask again.**

**Get help** when:

- there is aggression — growling, snapping, biting — towards people or animals;  
- there is severe fear, panic, or a dog who cannot settle;  
- there are repetitive behaviours you cannot interrupt (spinning, tail chasing, licking sores);  
- the dog is distressed when left alone (Chapter 9 covers prevention, not cure);  
- you are out of your depth despite honest prevention and simplifying;  
- anyone is frightened, or anyone is at risk.

Qualified reward-based professionals exist because some puzzles need eyes on the ground, not another chapter read at midnight by torchlight. Getting help is **success** — you noticed the limit of a book and a kitchen. Chapter 14 gathers the full five-check table, vet-first rules, and how to find someone qualified in the UK.

Multi-dog households, toddlers, and shared care are not moral failures. They are **context** on hard mode. Progress may look like “we managed safely today” more often than “we filmed a trick.” That still counts. The method scales down as readily as up: smaller world, shorter session, more gates, earlier stop.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A book cannot see your dog’s eyes, gait, or household.  
- **Do:** Use prevention — gates, distance, leads.  
- **Do:** Call the vet to rule out medical causes.  
- **Do:** Contact a qualified, reward-based professional when behaviour is risky or stuck.  
- **Don't:** Drill fear.  
- **Don't:** Borrow dominance stories from the internet.  
- **Don't:** Treat getting help as personal failure.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Safety and calm come before polish.  
- **Do:** Let training feel like relief, not siege.  
- **Don't:** Let fight-or-flight become daily life.

## Section 4 — What “progress” really looks like

Owners often measure progress by the hardest thing the dog can do on their best day. A fairer measure is how the **ordinary days** feel. Ask yourself, every few weeks:

- Is the house calmer than it was?  
- Do I shout less?  
- Does the dog look at me more often on walks, without being asked?  
- Do we have fewer “disaster” moments, and do they end sooner?  
- Do I stop sessions earlier, before things go wrong?

If most of those are “yes”, you are making progress — even if the park recall is still a work in progress. Behaviour change in a real household is often measured in **fewer bad moments** rather than more perfect ones.

## Practice lab — Two good goes and out

**You need:** a skill the dog already knows well, at an easy level. Not a new trick.

**Try this:**

1. Set up for success.  
2. Two clean goes: cue once, mark, treat, release.  
3. **Out.** Treats away, a cheerful word, done.

**Look for:** how it feels to stop early. That feeling is the product.

**If it’s not working:** if you cannot get two clean goes, you are not at the easy level yet. Shrink the world again.

**Stop if:** you notice you want “just one more”. That is exactly when to stop.

Try this on a day you are tired or cross — the days that matter. Two goes and out is an act of discipline. You are practising **not** taking a mood out on the dog. That is progress too.

## Troubleshooting

- **“We went backwards.”** → Check the usual suspects table. Re-teach one easy go. Check health.  
- **“I need him perfect before guests arrive.”** → Prevent instead: gate, lead, chew in another room. Train after, smaller.  
- **“Treats stopped working.”** → Worry or a full tummy. Comfort check. Change the reward or the place.  
- **“My partner undoes all my training.”** → Probably by accident. Agree one word list and one or two house rules. Show them, don’t lecture them.

## Stop if

- Behaviour is dangerous to people or other animals. Prevent and get help.  
- You are getting cross. Stop before the dog learns you are not safe when frustrated.  
- Vet red flags — appetite change, limping, sudden fear. Pause training.
