## Opening

You have a loop, cues that mean something, and a dog who sometimes cooperates like a dream and sometimes like a teenager with headphones. Progress in real kitchens is not a straight line — it is a scribble with good days, weird days, and the occasional “what happened to the sit we had on Tuesday?”

This chapter is owner-side progression: when to consolidate instead of chasing novelty, how to read setbacks without escalation, and when **simplifying** is success and **referring** is wisdom — not defeat.

If Chapter 4 gave you the loop and Chapter 5 gave you criteria, Chapter 6 gives you **permission** — to repeat, to pause, to call the vet, to hire help. Progress without pressure is not laziness; it is how skilled trainers avoid burning trust on a Tuesday.

## Section 1 — Consolidation beats novelty

Fluency is boring. It is also the point. Five clean sits in the hallway with one cue and happy pay beats a dozen new tricks performed once on video. **Consolidation** — paid reps at the current level until easy — is how behaviours survive bad moods and busy parks.

Boredom in the human is not a signal to add three Ds and a circus. It is often a signal to **generalise one step**, play a engagement game, or end session and live your life. Dogs do not need a new command weekly; they need reliable basics that still work when you are tired.

Consolidation also protects **you** on bad days. When the sit is fluent, you spend less cognitive budget during the doorbell crisis. That is the payoff for repetitive, unglamorous reps — not a ribbon, but headroom.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Novelty is shiny; fluency is what you need when the doorbell rings.  
- **Do:** Bank reps at the current criterion; celebrate “boring” success.  
- **Don't:** Escalate difficulty because you are restless; call the dog “stubborn” when you skipped consolidation.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Repetition with pay makes behaviour sturdy.  
- **Do:** Repeat what works at this level until it is easy, then change one thing.  
- **Don't:** Learn a graveyard of half-taught tricks; learn that every session must be harder.

## Section 2 — Setbacks: environment, health, adolescence

Setbacks have résumés. **Environment** changed — builders, new baby, different walk route. **Health** — sore ear, gut upset, ageing joints — makes everything harder and sometimes look like “bad behaviour.” **Adolescence** is not myth; brains remodel, priorities shift, yesterday’s star pupil discovers squirrels have opinions.

Your job when progress wobbles is not to train **angry**. It is to ask: manage, simplify, vet, or wait? Pain and sudden personality shifts deserve a **vet first** conversation before more drilling. Adolescent chaos responds to the same kind methods with **smaller worlds** and more patience — not moral lectures.

Recall the mantra: **make it easier — then ask again.** Shrink the world until one clean rep is possible (`claim-dt-pr-004` — progression via achievable steps, not pressure).

Adolescence deserves its own footnote of mercy: the dog who nailed recall at five months may audition for independence at twelve. Same method, slower tempo, more management, fewer street sermons. You are not starting from zero; you are re-banking trust while the brain remodels. Owners who interpret regression as defiance often escalate; owners who shrink the world often sail through with the relationship intact.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Setbacks are often context or biology, not betrayal.  
- **Do:** Check health when change is sudden; reduce criteria; manage what you cannot train today.  
- **Don't:** Train through pain; punish regression; match adolescent energy with your worst Tuesday.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** When the body or world is wrong, “try harder” is nonsense.  
- **Do:** Succeed small again; trust the loop in quieter conditions.  
- **Don't:** Learn that humans get scary when life is hard.

## Section 3 — When to simplify vs when to refer

**Simplify** when: setup was too hard, reward too weak, criterion muddy, comfort shaky, context jumped too far. That is most bad sessions. Fix the plan, not the dog.

**Refer** when: aggression, severe fear, compulsive patterns, repeated bites, or you are out of your depth despite honest management. Qualified reward-based professionals exist because some puzzles need eyes on the ground (`claim-imdt-003-referral`). Referral is **success** — you noticed the limit of a book and a kitchen.

Chapter 14 gathers the full five-check table, vet-first rules, and UK signposts. For now: if simplification and management do not restore safety or calm, stop rehearsing the problem and ask for help.

Multi-dog households, toddlers, and shared custody are not moral failures — they are **context** on hard mode. Progress may look like “we managed safely today” more often than “we filmed a trick.” That still counts. The book’s method scales down as readily as up: smaller world, shorter session, more gates, earlier stop.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A book cannot see your dog’s eyes, gait, or household.  
- **Do:** Use management; call the vet for medical rule-outs; contact ABTC-registered or force-free professionals when behaviour is risky or stuck.  
- **Don't:** Drill fear; borrow dominance stories from the internet; treat referral as personal failure.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Safety and calm come before polish.  
- **Do:** Experience training as relief, not siege.  
- **Don't:** Rehearse fight-or-flight as daily life.

## Practice lab — Two good reps and out

Pick a **known** skill at an easy level — not a new trick.

1. Setup for success.  
2. Two clean reps: cue once, mark, pay, release.  
3. **Out.** Treats away, praise, done.

Notice how it feels to stop early. That feeling is the product. If you cannot get two clean reps, you are not at the easy level yet — shrink again.

Try this on a day you are tired or cross — the days that matter. Two reps and out is an act of discipline: you are practising **not** taking a mood out on the dog. That is progression too.

## Troubleshooting

- **“We went backwards.”** → Often context or age. Re-teach one easy rep; check health.  
- **“I need him perfect before guests arrive.”** → Manage guests with gates and leads; train after, smaller.  
- **“Treats stopped working.”** → Stress or satiation. Comfort check; change reward or location.

## Stop if

- Behaviour is dangerous to people or other animals — manage and refer.  
- You are escalating — stop before the dog learns you are not safe when frustrated.  
- Vet red flags — appetite change, lameness, sudden fear — pause training.
