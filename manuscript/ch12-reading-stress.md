## Opening

You have met the teaching loop, the reward menu, and the owner mantra. Good. Before we ask your dog to recall past squirrels, tolerate door chaos, or walk past a pub garden full of crisps, we need one skill that is not a trick: **noticing when the lesson is too much**.

This chapter is **stress literacy**, not diagnosis. You are not playing vet, behaviourist, or TikTok body-language detective. You are learning to see **clusters** of discomfort, take them seriously, and change the plan before frustration — yours or theirs — becomes the training method. Part III assumes you will run a **comfort check** whenever live life gets loud. If you skip this chapter and jump to recall, you will train through worry and wonder why trust evaporated.

**Bookmark this chapter** if your dog freezes, won't take food outside, or looks worried on walks — read it **before** heavy work on come, lead, or doors.

## Section 1 — What stress looks like in pet dogs (clusters, not pixels)

Stress in pet dogs rarely arrives as a single cartoon signal. One yawn might be a tired Tuesday. One lip lick might be anticipation of cheese. What matters is **context plus a cluster**: several signs together, when the situation is genuinely demanding — vet waiting room, crowded pavement, stranger leaning over the garden gate.

Common clusters owners notice at home and on walks include:

- **Body tension:** stiff posture, weight shifted back, tail held low or tight (breed-dependent — compare to *this* dog’s usual loose wiggle).  
- **Face and mouth:** panting without heat or exercise, tight closed mouth, whale eye (whites showing), repeated lip licks or nose licks.  
- **Movement choices:** freezing, backing away, trying to leave, hiding behind your legs, sudden sniffing the ground as if the pavement is fascinating (displacement).  
- **Vocalising:** whining, barking that sounds “tight” rather than playful, growling (information, not insult).  
- **Shut-down:** stillness, refusing food they normally love, “going blank” when touched.

None of these alone proves panic. **Clusters in context** tell you the dog’s nervous system is working overtime. UK welfare guidance consistently warns against training that relies on fear or pain; noticing stress early is how you stay on the reward-based side of that line without needing a philosophy lecture mid-walk.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Stress is **information about the setup**, not a moral failing. Your job is to believe what you see, even when Auntie insists “he’s fine, he just needs confidence.”  
- **Do:** Name what you notice in plain language (“stiff, won’t eat, backing up”) and **pause the lesson** — shorter distance, quieter street, or full stop.  
- **Don't:** Fixate on one pixel from an internet chart. train “through it” because you paid for a six-week course. punish growling and call it manners.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Dogs communicate discomfort long before bites. Growling and stiffening are **requests for space**, not disrespect.  
- **Do:** Let “I need out” be a valid answer — move away, end the session, manage next time.  
- **Don't:** Learn that warning signals are ignored until explosion is the only option left.

## Section 2 — Your dog vs the internet chart

Social media loves a single photo with arrows pointing at ears. Real dogs are individuals: breed, age, coat, history, and today’s sleep all change the picture. A rescue who went stiff on day three is not “dominant”; an adolescent who suddenly won’t enter the park may be overwhelmed, not stubborn.

Use charity and vet-trusted explainers as **orientation**, not scorecards. Useful starting points (check current URLs when you read):

- [Blue Cross — dog advice](https://www.bluecross.org.uk/advice/dog)  
- [PDSA — dog behaviour and body language](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/looking-after-your-pet/puppies-dogs-and-young-dogs/dog-behaviour)

Compare your dog **to their own baseline**: how do they greet you when relaxed? How do they look on a boring Tuesday evening? That calm photo is your reference frame.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Charts help you **ask better questions**. they do not replace your vet or a qualified behaviourist when fear or aggression is frequent.  
- **Do:** Film ten seconds of a tricky moment (safely, from distance) and watch without sound — often you see tension you missed live.  
- **Don't:** Label the dog “anxious” as identity. guilt-spiral because a stranger’s collie looks “happier”. diagnose medical pain from a blog post.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They are not performing for your camera. they are responding to pressure in the environment.  
- **Do:** Build trust by **shrinking the world** until their body loosens again — same method as harder skills, different goal.  
- **Don't:** Flood them with “socialisation” to fix fear. pair every scary moment with shouted cues.  

## Section 3 — Stop, simplify, manage (comfort in the five checks)

When something stalls in training, Chapter 4 and Chapter 14 ask you to run **five checks**: setup, reward, criterion, **comfort**, context. This chapter owns **comfort**.

If you see a stress cluster:

1. **Stop** — no more reps, no louder recall, no “just one more guest.”  
2. **Simplify** — easier version of the skill or abandon teaching for today.  
3. **Manage** — lead, gate, distance, separate room — so the dog does not rehearse panic while you regroup.

Say the mantra: **“Make it easier — then ask again.”** Sometimes “easier” means **not asking at all** until you have a plan, a vet check, or professional support.

Comfort is not softness. It is **safety for learning**. A dog who cannot eat chicken on the pavement is telling you the criterion or context is wrong — not that chicken is broken.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Ending early is a **success metric**, especially before Part III. Impatience is your cue to shrink the world, not to push.  
- **Do:** Write one line after a hard outing: “Comfort check failed at ___. next time I will ___.”.  
- **Don't:** Treat stop rules as defeat. keep score of “sessions finished while still okay” instead of “minutes drilled.”.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They learn whether you are safe to be honest with — or whether they must endure to the end of the walk.  
- **Do:** Experience relief when you retreat. pair your presence with **exit ramps** from pressure.  
- **Don't:** Learn that stress must be ignored to keep you happy.

## Observation exercise (not a practice lab)

**Five-minute calm watch.** No training, no cues, no phone scroll. Sit where your dog can choose distance. Note baseline: breathing, posture, how they hold tail and ears at rest. If something in the environment shifts (delivery, neighbour dog), note **clusters** only. Stop at five minutes. You are practising **noticing**, not fixing.

## Troubleshooting

- **If you only see stress on walks, not at home** → Context check (five checks #5). Start teaching on quieter routes; vet rule-out if sudden change.  
- **If you are unsure whether it is pain** → Vet first. Lameness, flinching on touch, appetite change — not a training problem.  
- **If everyone says “let him work it out”** → Management and distance are legitimate tools. Flooding is not this book’s method.

## Stop if

- Growling, snapping, biting, or repeated attempts to flee — **stop**, manage safety, see Chapter 14.  
- You feel yourself speeding up, shouting, or gripping equipment white-knuckled — **stop**; no dog learns well from that version of you.  
- Stress signs persist daily despite simplified setups — professional and/or veterinary help, not more reps.
