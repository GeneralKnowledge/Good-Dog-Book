# Chapter 8 — Settle and calm at home

## Opening

The doorbell, the kettle, the children cartwheeling past the sofa — home is not a monastery. Yet most households want something simple: a dog who can **switch off** sometimes, without being shouted into stillness or pinned to the floor in the name of calm.

**Settle** in this book means **invited rest with pay**, not suppressed alertness. You are teaching a landing pad (often a mat) and a rest spot the dog can choose, with clear stop rules from Chapter 12 when the room is too much. Short sessions beat marathon “calm down” battles — welfare guidance favours brief, positive training over endless drilling.

**Bookmark this chapter** for calm at home — it supports **come** and door chaos, but you do not need a show-ring settle to have a good pet.

## Section 1 — Mat as optional landing pad

A mat is a portable cue: “here is a place where four paws and a relaxed body earn gentle rewards.” The dog may stand, sit, or lie — you are building **duration of calm-ish behaviour**, not a statue competition. Start in a boring room with no audience.

**Teaching sketch (reward-based):**

1. Place mat; when the dog investigates, mark and reward.  
2. Reward any contact with the mat; pause briefly between pays so they stay rather than snatch-and-leave.  
3. Add a calm cue word only when they offer stillness often — one cue, one meaning.  
4. Release to sniff or play so the mat is not a trap.

**Duration in honest seconds** — not in your head while you scroll:

1. **One second of stillness** (any relaxed posture on the mat) → mark, pay, release. Repeat until boring.  
2. **Two seconds** — same rule; if they pop up, they pop up; no scolding. Reset with a fresh invite.  
3. **Five seconds** — only when two seconds is easy most reps; still release often.  
4. **Add one boring variable** — you step one pace away and return, or the kettle clicks once in the next room — not both on the same day.  
5. **Stop while the mat still looks like a good deal** — three to five minutes total, or fewer reps if the room is noisy.

Keep sessions **short** — a handful of successes beats marathon “calm down” battles. If they leave, they leave; invite again later. Forced pinning teaches nothing useful and erodes trust.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Duration grows in **tiny increments**. your impatience is the usual reason “settle” becomes a fight.  
- **Do:** Count successes, not minutes. use a release word so “off mat” is legal and fun.  
- **Don't:** Train settle while you are cooking a stressful dinner. punish leaving the mat. expect adult nap length on day one.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Mat means “good things happen when I hang out here.”  
- **Do:** Discover that stillness pays better than pacing. learn a clear end to the exercise.  
- **Don't:** Learn that “settle” means “trapped until human is less angry.”

## Section 2 — Calm around movement — honest expectations

Dogs notice movement. Post arrives, curtains twitch, a housemate booms down the stairs — alertness is **normal**, not naughtiness. Your goal is not a dog who ignores life; it is a dog who can **recover** and who is not rehearsing frantic barking every afternoon.

Teach in **graded steps**: one person walks past at distance while you pay calm checks on the mat; or reward quiet watching before the dog launches into commentary. If barking already happens, **manage** (baby gate, closed door, cover window) while you teach — do not yell over the noise and call it training.

Reward **recovery**: the moment they soften after a startle is worth a mark. You are reinforcing the return to baseline, not punishing the alert.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “Calm” is a **behaviour you reinforce**, not a vibe you demand. Television dogs are edited.  
- **Do:** Lower excitement in the room when practising. one movement trigger per session.  
- **Don't:** Punish alertness. rehearse chaos daily without management. train through shutdown (see Chapter 12).  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Movement predicts different things in different houses — sometimes play, sometimes nothing.  
- **Do:** Learn that quiet watching or softening after a bark can pay. practise in easy versions first.  
- **Don't:** Learn that barking is the only way to make the post disappear when shouting is the main consequence.

## Section 3 — Rest spot and choice to leave

Beyond the mat, give a **rest spot** the dog owns: crate (door open), bed in a quiet corner, behind a gate. Rule: when they are there, **no poking** — especially children. The dog must be able to **leave** if they choose; blocking exit turns rest into stress.

Household realism: teach humans the script. “Dog on bed = we walk past.” Pair with management when visitors or chaos exceed what your dog can handle today. Settle skills support door and guest chapters later; they are not a substitute for gates and leads when excitement spikes.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Choice reduces bite risk and burnout. forced “cuddles” on the rest spot undo the lesson.  
- **Do:** Post a family rule on the fridge. supervise kid–dog contact until settle is reliable.  
- **Don't:** Drag the dog to the bed for a photo. train when the dog is already over threshold.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Rest spot means predictable quiet and escape from hassle.  
- **Do:** Choose to rest when tired. trust that leaving is allowed.  
- **Don't:** Learn that beds are ambush sites for grabbing and loud handling.

## Practice lab — Mat settle, three minutes max

Quiet room. Mat down. Treat pouch. Nobody practising cartwheels nearby.

1. **Contact pays** — dog sniffs or steps on mat → mark, treat, pause half a second. Three to five reps.  
2. **One-second stillness** — mark any relaxed pause on the mat → pay → **release word** → let them off to sniff. Three reps.  
3. **Optional stretch** — if stillness was easy, try **two seconds** once; then release and end.  
4. **Comfort check** — loose body, takes food, can leave? Good. Won’t eat, panting, or you are repeating “settle”? **Stop.** Put mat away; try tomorrow.

**Make it easier — then ask again:** if stillness fails twice, go back to contact-only pays or a smaller mat in a duller corner.

## A note on “calm down” language

Owners often say **calm down** when they mean **stop bothering me**. The dog hears volume and tension, not a taught skill. Swap the phrase for something you have actually trained — mat, rest spot, or quiet praise for softening — and manage the environment when you cannot teach. Forced stillness (holding, pinning, shouting until quiet) may produce silence while stress rises; Chapter 12’s comfort check catches that mismatch.

## Troubleshooting

- **If settle only works with you in the room** → Normal early on; add short exits, return, pay calm — one variable at a time (Chapter 5).  
- **If the dog erupts when the door goes** → Management first; settle is parallel work, not the door fix (Chapter 11).  
- **If they won’t settle at all** → Vet check (pain, itch, sleep); shorten sessions; check reward value.

## Stop if

- Growling when approached on rest spot — stop contact; manage space; see Chapter 14.  
- Panting, shaking, or refusing food on the mat in a “quiet” room — comfort check failed; simplify or stop.  
- You are using the mat as punishment (“go to your place” after shouting) — pause; rebuild with invited, paid calm.

## Before the next chapter

Home calm makes **recall and lead work** easier — a dog who can recover indoors copes better outdoors. Chapter 9 tackles coming back when it matters; bring your mat mindset: trust is built in small, cheerful payments, not in one heroic afternoon.

If your household is chaotic today, settle is still worth one **micro-session** — thirty seconds on the mat with a single treat — so both of you remember what calm feels like before the week runs away from you.
