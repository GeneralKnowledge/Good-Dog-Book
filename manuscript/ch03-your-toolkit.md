## Opening

You understand how dogs learn. Now you need a workbench. Not a garage full of gadgets — a **reward menu**, a marker that means something, a word that means “you’re free”, and a place quiet enough that your dog’s first impression of training is “oh, we win here,” not “why is Mum doing that voice again?”

This chapter’s promise is modest and powerful: a **three-minute session** almost anywhere sensible, ending while you both still fancy another go. If you can do that, Part II’s teaching loop will feel like a routine instead of chaos.

Think of yourself as a stage manager, not a sergeant major. Your job before the curtain rises is mundane and decisive: pockets checked, distractions reduced, rewards ranked, marker charged. The dog’s job is simply to show up and collect wins. When those roles are clear, training stops feeling like a moral exam.

You do not need every gadget in the pet shop. **Bookmark this chapter** for the day treats “stop working” — usually the place or the pay, not a broken dog.

## Section 1 — Reward menus (food, play, access)

Rewards are not bribes if you use them **after** the behaviour, as pay. The bribe mistake is waving sausage at a dog who will not come, then paying for the standoff. The skilled move is ranking what **this dog, today, in this room** cares about.

Build a menu:

- **Food** — pea-sized, swallow-fast. Save “jackpot” tiers for hard work or new places.  
- **Play** — a short tug, a tossed toy, a silly chase — if it genuinely lights up *this* dog.  
- **Access** — “go sniff,” door opens, greet a friend, hop on the sofa — if that is what they were working for.  
- **Calm** — for some worried dogs, being allowed to move *away* from something is the best pay of all.

Value changes with place. Cheese in the kitchen may lose to squirrels in the park. That is not insulting. It is information. Change the pay grade or make the place easier — not your dignity.

A practical habit: before you ask for anything, ask yourself, *“Would I get out of bed for this?”* If the answer is probably not, your dog’s answer will match. Jackpots are not bribery. They are honest wages for harder work. Saving the best stuff for the hardest jobs keeps the menu meaningful instead of turning every session into a clearance sale on dry biscuits.

### Making your own menu

Try this once, on a quiet afternoon. Put five different treats in five little piles on a plate: kibble, a shop-bought training treat, cheese, cooked chicken, and something weird your dog adores (some dogs would sell their soul for a frozen pea). Offer two at a time, one in each open hand, and see which they pick first. Swap them around. In ten minutes you will have a rough league table.

Now write it down — fridge, phone, back of an envelope:

| Tier | Example | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Everyday** | Kibble, dry treats | Easy things at home |
| **Good** | Training treats, cheese | New skills, the garden |
| **Jackpot** | Chicken, sausage, sprats | Outside, hard moments, recall |

Count treats as part of the daily food allowance, especially with small dogs. If training days are big days, use some of their dinner for the easy stuff.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Value depends on the place. Rank rewards **before** the session, not after a flop.  
- **Do:** Have the right tier ready for where you are training.  
- **Do:** Vary the menu so life stays interesting.  
- **Don't:** Bribe after refusal.  
- **Don't:** Pay for ignoring you, then wonder why ignoring repeats.  
- **Don't:** Make food the only option if play or access matters more to this dog today.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Payday drives effort. Effort is not moral. It is economics with a wag.  
- **Do:** Discover what counts as a jackpot. Sometimes it is tripe. Sometimes it is “keys jingling means a walk.”  
- **Don't:** Ask for steak-level focus while paying in kibble.  
- **Don't:** Let jumping on you become the way treats appear.

## Section 2 — Marker word or click — mechanics without frenzy

A **marker** bridges the gap between the behaviour and the treat. Clickers are crisp. Words like “yes” are portable and you never leave them at home. Pick one system for now. This book says “mark” to mean either.

Choose a word you do not use all day. “Good” is everywhere in normal speech, so it gets diluted. “Yes”, “yep”, or a short word of your own is better. Say it in a calm, normal voice. The marker is information, not a celebration.

**Charge the marker** before you use it for real: mark, then treat. Mark, then treat. Ten to fifteen times, no cue, no behaviour required. The dog should brighten at the sound: *something good is coming*. Quiet marker, calm hands. You are building a prediction, not hosting a fireworks show.

Pair the marker with kindness. If clicks happen only during scary handling, the click becomes a warning siren. If marks arrive during calm focus games, they become welcome news.

Mechanics, without frenzy: still hands, treat delivered to the mouth without chasing the dog, marker **before** you reach into the bag. If you mark and then spend four seconds unwrapping, the dog learns to stare at your fingers, not at their own behaviour. Pre-open a few treats. Fumble later on your own time.

### Where the treat lands matters

Where you deliver the treat teaches as much as when.

- Want the dog to **stay** where they are? Deliver the treat to them, in that position.  
- Want them to **reset** for another go? Toss the treat a short way, so they move off and come back.  
- Want them **at your side** on a walk? Deliver the treat by your leg, at their nose height.  
- Want them **calm**? Place the treat slowly between their paws. Do not throw it.

You will see this idea again in nearly every chapter. A treat thrown in the air builds excitement. A treat placed quietly on the floor builds calm.

Professional guidance in the UK reward-based world is blunt: **no training need requires pain, fear, or intimidation**. Your toolkit should not include shouting as strategy, collar jerks as punctuation, or “dominance” theatre. If a tool makes the dog flinch, it is not in this book’s kit.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Charge the marker **before** behaviour work. Same as checking batteries before a torch walk.  
- **Do:** One mark, one treat.  
- **Do:** Keep your body still and your sessions short.  
- **Don't:** Click storm because you are excited.  
- **Don't:** Mark while fumbling the bag. It teaches sloppiness.  
- **Don't:** Pair the marker with rough grabs or scary noises.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The sound predicts a treat.  
- **Do:** Charge with ten to fifteen pairs until their ears perk up.  
- **Do:** Use it on easy wins only at first.  
- **Don't:** Let the marker become linked with fear.  
- **Don't:** Leave them guessing which behaviour earned the treat.

## Section 3 — The release word: “you’re free”

The marker says *that was right*. The **release word** says *you can stop now*. Owners often skip it and then wonder why “sit” lasts as long as it takes to say it.

Pick a word: “free”, “break”, “okay”, or “off you go”. Say it, then encourage the dog to move — step back, toss a treat away from them, or invite them to follow you. After a few sessions, the word itself tells them the job is done.

Why it matters: without a release, the dog decides when the sit ends. With one, *you* do, and the dog always knows where they stand (or sit). Later, when you build longer settles and stays (Chapters 5 and 9), the release word is what makes duration fair.

Use it every time. A sit, a down, a mat settle — all end with the same word. Do not let “good” be both the marker and the release, or the dog will get up the moment you praise them.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** You are responsible for ending each behaviour, not the dog.  
- **Do:** Release every time, even when it feels fussy.  
- **Don't:** Walk off and leave the dog in a sit, wondering if they are still on duty.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The release word means “the job is done”.  
- **Do:** Learn that the release is a good thing too — often followed by a game or a sniff.  
- **Don't:** Learn that ending the behaviour is something they decide on their own.

## Section 4 — A starter behaviour: the hand touch

Every toolkit needs one easy, cheerful behaviour you can ask for anywhere. The **hand touch** — the dog bumping their nose to your open palm — is ideal. It is quick to teach, hard to get wrong, and useful for moving a dog without grabbing their collar.

1. Hold your flat hand a few centimetres from the dog’s nose, palm towards them. Many dogs sniff it straight away.  
2. The instant their nose touches your palm, **mark**. Then deliver the treat from your *other* hand.  
3. Take your hand away. Offer it again. Repeat five times, then stop.  
4. Over the next few sessions, move your hand a little left, right, or higher, so they have to move to touch it.  
5. When they are touching reliably, say “touch” just before you offer the hand.

If the dog does not touch, rub a little treat smell on your palm. If they lick or mouth, mark the moment just before — when the nose arrives.

You will use the hand touch in later chapters to bring a dog to your side on walks, to call them away from the door, and as an easy win when a session is going wrong.

## Section 5 — Where to train first; prevent chaos when the world is too loud

Place is half the lesson. The hallway beats the high street. The garden beats the dog park. **Prevention** — gates, leads, choosing quiet times — is how you stop rehearsing disaster while you teach.

When the world is too loud, you are not failing. You are **designing**. Baby steps are not babyish. They are how fluent dogs happen. Testing a new skill on the busiest pavement on day one is how people conclude their dog is “spiteful” when the dog is simply **learning in a casino**.

If visitors, builders, or small humans turn the kitchen into a funfair, train elsewhere or skip training and **prevent** the wrong practice (gate up, lead on, or a calm nap instead). A skipped session beats a bad one.

### When to train

Timing matters as much as place.

- **Good times:** after a short walk and a sniff, when the dog is awake but not wild. A little before a meal, so food matters.  
- **Bad times:** straight after a big run, when they are exhausted. Straight after a fright. When you are rushing, hungry, or cross.  
- **Best habit:** a few tiny sessions a day, stuck onto things you already do. Waiting for the kettle. Adverts. Before you put your shoes on.

**Session length:** short, positive sessions beat grinding. Set a timer if you must — three minutes is a feature, not an apology. Ending while the dog is still keen teaches them that training is a highlight, not a sentence. Put the treat bag away **before** they wander off bored. You want them mildly offended that the fun stopped.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** The environment is a dial, not a virtue test. You control more of it than you think.  
- **Do:** Hallway before park. One distraction at a time, later.  
- **Do:** Use leads and gates so “don’t practise the wrong thing” is a plan.  
- **Don't:** Test new skills on the busiest pavement because you are embarrassed.  
- **Don't:** Blame the dog for a lesson you taught in chaos.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Success in quiet builds confidence for later noise.  
- **Do:** Win where winning is likely.  
- **Don't:** Let them practise failing in overexcitement.  
- **Don't:** Let training become linked with overwhelm.

## Section 6 — One household, one dictionary

Dogs are confused by a house where “down” means lie down to one person and get off the sofa to another. Spend five minutes agreeing your words. Stick them on the fridge:

| Meaning | Our word |
| --- | --- |
| That was right (marker) | *yes* |
| You’re free | *free* |
| Bottom on floor | *sit* |
| Lie down | *down* |
| Get off the furniture | *off* |
| Come to me | *(your recall word — Chapter 10)* |
| Touch my hand | *touch* |

Children can join in with easy jobs (marking a sit, scattering treats) once an adult has set things up. Nobody in the house should be using pain, fear, or telling-off to train. If someone is, that conversation matters more than any skill in this book.

## Practice lab — Charge marker + one easy win

Three to five minutes. Stop while the dog is keen.

**You need:** a quiet room, 20 small treats, your marker word.

**Try this:**

1. Charge the marker: 10–15 mark-then-treat pairs, no cue.  
2. Pick one easy behaviour — a sit they already know, or the hand touch from Section 4. Ask once (or show them with your hand once). Mark the instant it happens. Treat. Release with your free word.  
3. Stop after **three to five wins**, not three to five minutes. Put the treats away while the dog still wants more.

**Look for:** ears and eyes brightening at the marker. A dog who stays near you hopefully after you stop.

**If it’s not working:** do not drill. Note one thing to make easier next time — a quieter room, a better treat, or an easier behaviour. Chapter 4 names the full routine.

**Stop if:** the dog will not take food, keeps moving away, or you notice your voice rising.

## Troubleshooting

- **Dog spits treats out** → Worry or a full stomach. Calmer spot, smellier food, or train later. See Chapter 7 if it persists.  
- **Clicker startles them** → Switch to a soft “yes”, muffle the click in a pocket, or charge from another room.  
- **Dog mugs your hands for treats** → Keep treats in a pouch or behind your back. Mark and treat only when the nose is away from your hand. Calm hands teach calm mouths.  
- **Dog snatches** → Offer treats on a flat palm or drop them on the floor. Snatching often settles when the dog stops worrying the treat will vanish.  
- **“I don’t have time.”** → Three minutes counts. Consistency beats heroics.

## Stop if

- You or the dog are getting sharp, silly, or sloppy. End on a win from two goes ago.  
- The dog will not take food in the training spot. Treat it as a comfort signal. Make the place easier, or pause.  
- You are tempted to add corrections “because treats aren’t working”. Revisit the place and the ask first.
