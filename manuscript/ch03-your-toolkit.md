## Opening

You understand how dogs learn; now you need a workbench. Not a garage full of gadgets — a **reward menu**, a marker that means something, and a place quiet enough that your dog’s first impression of training is “oh, we win here,” not “why is Mum doing that voice again?”

This chapter’s promise is modest and powerful: a **three-minute session** almost anywhere sensible, ending while you both still fancy another go. If you can do that, Part II’s teaching loop will feel like choreography instead of chaos.

Think of yourself as a stage manager, not a sergeant major. Your job before the curtain rises is mundane and decisive: pockets checked, distractions reduced, reward ranked, marker charged. The dog’s job is simply to show up and collect wins. When those roles are clear, training stops feeling like a moral exam.

You do not need every gadget in the pet shop. **Bookmark this chapter** when treats “stop working” — usually place or pay, not a broken dog.

## Section 1 — Reward menus (food, play, access)

Rewards are not bribes if you use them **before** refusal as setup and **after** behaviour as pay. The bribe mistake is waving sausage at a dog who will not come, then paying for the standoff. The skilled move is ranking what **this dog, today, in this room** cares about.

Build a menu:

- **Food** — pea-sized, swallow-fast; save “jackpot” tiers for hard reps or new places.  
- **Play** — a short tug, a tossed toy, a silly chase — if it genuinely energises *this* dog.  
- **Access** — “go sniff,” door opens, greet a friend, hop on the sofa — if that is what they were working for.

Value changes with context. Cheese in the kitchen may lose to squirrels in the park. That is not insulting; it is information. Change the pay grade or shrink the world — not your dignity.

A practical habit: before you cue, ask *“Would I get out of bed for this?”* If the answer is probably not, your dog’s answer will match. Jackpots are not bribery; they are honest wages for harder work. Saving the best stuff for the hardest reps keeps the menu meaningful instead of turning every session into a clearance sale on dry biscuits.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Value is contextual. Rank rewards **before** the session, not after a flop.  
- **Do:** Keep treats visible only as part of the pay ritual. vary the menu so life stays interesting.  
- **Don't:** Bribe after refusal. pay for ignoring you then wonder why ignoring repeats. use food as the only love language if play or access motivates more today.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Payday drives effort. Effort is not moral. it is economics with a wag.  
- **Do:** Discover what counts as a jackpot — sometimes it is tripe, sometimes it is “keys jingling = walk.”  
- **Don't:** Learn that only kibble exists when steak-level focus is needed. learn that jumping on you is how treats appear.  

## Section 2 — Marker word or click — mechanics without frenzy

A **marker** bridges the gap between behaviour and treat delivery. Clickers are crisp; words like “yes” are portable. Pick one system for now.

**Charge the marker** before you ask for obedience theatre: mark → treat, mark → treat, ten to fifteen times, no cue required. The dog should brighten at the sound: *something good is coming*. Quiet marker, calm hands. You are building a prediction, not hosting a fireworks show.

Pair the marker with kindness. If clicks happen only during scary handling, the click becomes a warning siren. If markers arrive during calm focus games, they become welcome news.

Mechanics, without frenzy: still hands, treat delivered to the mouth without chasing the dog, marker **before** you root in the bag. If you mark then spend four seconds unwrapping, the dog learns to stare at your fingers, not their own behaviour. Pre-open a few treats; fumble later on your own time.

Professional guidance in the UK reward-based world is blunt: **no training need requires pain, fear, or intimidation** (`claim-abtc-003-no-aversives`). Your toolkit should not include shouting as strategy, collar pops as punctuation, or “dominance” theatre. If a tool makes the dog flinch, it is not in this book’s kit.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Charge the marker **before** behaviour work — same as checking batteries before a torch walk.  
- **Do:** One marker, one treat. still body. sessions short (RSPCA-aligned advice favours brief, happy reps over marathons).  
- **Don't:** Click storm because you are excited. mark while fumbling the bag and teach sloppiness. pair the marker with rough grabs or scary noise.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The sound predicts pay — classical conditioning in plain English.  
- **Do:** Ten to fifteen charge reps until ears perk. then use on easy wins only at first.  
- **Don't:** Associate marker with fear. hear “yes” and still not know which behaviour paid.  

## Section 3 — Where to train first; management when the world is too loud

Environment is half the lesson. The hallway beats the high street. The garden beats the dog park. **Management** — gates, leads, choosing quiet times — is how you stop rehearsing disaster while you teach.

When the world is too loud, you are not failing. You are **designing**. Baby steps are not babyish; they are how fluent dogs happen. Proofing on day one is how people conclude their dog is “spiteful” when the dog is simply **learning in a casino**.

If visitors, builders, or small humans turn the kitchen into a funfair, train elsewhere or skip training and manage. A skipped session beats a bad one.

**Session length:** RSPCA-aligned guidance favours short, positive reps over grinding (`claim-rspca-002-short-sessions`). Set a timer if you must — three minutes is a feature, not an apology. Ending while eager teaches the dog that training is a highlight, not a sentence. Put the treat bag away **before** they walk off bored; you want them mildly offended that the fun stopped.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Environment is a dial, not a virtue test. You control more of it than you think.  
- **Do:** Hallway before park. one distraction at a time later. use leads and gates so “don’t practice the wrong thing” is a plan.  
- **Don't:** “Proof” on the busiest pavement because you are embarrassed. blame the dog for a lesson you taught in chaos.  

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Success in quiet builds confidence for later noise.  
- **Do:** Win where winning is likely. generalise with intention in later chapters.  
- **Don't:** Learn failure loops in overstimulation. associate training with overwhelm.  

## Practice lab — Charge marker + one easy win

Three to five minutes, stop while eager.

1. Charge marker: 10–15 mark → treat pairs, no cue.  
2. One easy behaviour you already have — sit, or a hand touch to your palm. Cue once (or lure gently once), mark the instant it happens, pay, **release** (“free” / break).  
3. Stop after **three to five wins**, not three to five minutes. Put treats away while the dog still wants to play.

If nothing happens, do not drill. Note one thing to make easier next time — distance, distraction, or reward tier. Chapter 4 names the full loop.

## Troubleshooting

- **Dog spits treats out** → Stress or full stomach. Calmer spot, smellier food, or train later. See comfort notes in Chapter 12 if it persists.  
- **Clicker startles them** → Switch to a soft “yes,” or muffle the click, or charge from another room.  
- **“I don’t have time.”** → Three minutes counts. Consistency beats heroics.

## Stop if

- You or the dog are getting sharp, silly, or sloppy — end on a win two reps ago.  
- The dog will not take food in the training spot — treat as a comfort signal; simplify environment or pause.  
- You are tempted to add corrections “because treats aren’t working” — revisit setup and criterion first.
