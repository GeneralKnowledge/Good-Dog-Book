## Opening

Same book, different tempo. A twelve-week puppy, a seven-month teenager, and a rescue on day twelve in your hallway are not three versions of the same checklist. They share one method — reward-based teaching, sensible prevention (gates, leads), the vet when health is in question, comfort checks when stress shows — but **expectations** and **first priorities** differ.

This chapter sorts the noise so you do not punish a rescue for missing “puppy class”, or treat a teenager like a finished adult. There is a short note on older dogs at the end, because they deserve a mention too.

**Bookmark this chapter** for puppy, teenage, or rescue tempo. The basics still apply. Sit, come, and walk nicely just move more slowly on some days.

## Section 1 — Puppies

### The first few days

Bringing a puppy home is exciting for you and enormous for them. They have left their mum, their littermates, and everything they know. Go gently.

- **Keep the first days quiet.** Family only. Visitors can wait a week.  
- **Set up a puppy area** with a bed, water, a few chews, and a gate. A crate with the door open can be a cosy den if it is introduced kindly.  
- **Sleep, sleep, sleep.** Young puppies need a huge amount of sleep — often most of the day. An overtired puppy is a bitey, wild puppy. If they are going mad in the evening, they probably need a nap, not more play.  
- **Night-time:** many puppies settle better at first with someone nearby — their bed by yours, or you sleeping downstairs for a few nights. You can move gradually later. A puppy crying alone in the dark is not learning independence; they are frightened.  
- **Start the vet relationship.** Vaccinations, worming, flea treatment, and a check-up. Ask your vet when it is safe to walk in public places.

### Toilet training

Toilet training is **prevention plus pay for outdoor success**. Puppies have small bladders and honest mistakes. Shouting teaches them to hide puddles, not to hold on.

1. **Take them out often.** After waking up, after eating, after playing, after any excitement, and every hour or so while awake at first. Last thing at night and first thing in the morning.  
2. **Same spot, same word.** Go to the same patch of garden. Stand quietly. Use a word like “busy” or “be quick” just as they start to go.  
3. **Pay success.** The moment they finish, quietly praise and give a treat, right there. Then a little play or a sniff, so going outside doesn’t mean the fun ends.  
4. **Supervise or prevent indoors.** When you can’t watch them, use the puppy area. Look out for sniffing and circling — that means “soon”. Calmly carry or lead them out.  
5. **Accidents happen.** Clean up with an enzyme cleaner (ordinary cleaners can leave a smell that draws them back). Say nothing to the puppy. If you catch them mid-wee, calmly scoop them up and take them outside. No telling off, no rubbing noses — it teaches them to be scared of going in front of you.

Most puppies are mostly reliable within a few months, but every puppy is different. If there are sudden setbacks, lots of accidents, or straining, see the vet.

### Mouthing and biting

**Mouthing** is normal play and exploration. Puppies use their mouths the way toddlers use their hands. Those teeth are also sharp. The aim is to teach them that human skin is very fragile, and that toys are the right thing to bite.

1. **Have toys everywhere.** When the puppy mouths your hands, calmly offer a toy instead. Make the toy wiggle. Pay with play when they bite the toy.  
2. **Pay soft mouths.** When stroking, if they are gentle, mark and treat.  
3. **If teeth stay on skin**, calmly stop playing. Stand up, fold your arms, or step behind the gate for a few seconds. Then come back and offer a toy.  
4. **Watch the clock.** Biting usually gets worse when the puppy is tired, over-excited, or hungry. That is often the cue for a nap, a chew, or dinner — not more training.  
5. **Supervise children.** Children waving hands and squealing is very exciting for puppies. Give kids a toy to offer, and a rule: “If the puppy bites, stand still like a tree and call a grown-up.”

Do not hold the mouth shut, tap the nose, or “bite back”. These can make puppies more frantic or frightened of hands. If biting is breaking skin regularly, or seems more than play, talk to your vet and a qualified trainer.

### Gentle exposure (getting used to the world)

Puppies go through a sensitive period early in life when new experiences make a strong impression. Good experiences now help a dog feel safe as an adult. Bad experiences can last a long time. That is why **how** you introduce the world matters more than **how much**.

Dogs Trust advises going at **your puppy’s pace**, with **rewards they like**. Every puppy is an individual. Welfare guidance says avoid frightening or harsh methods.

- **Quality over quantity.** One calm, positive experience beats ten overwhelming ones.  
- **Distance and choice.** Watch the world from far enough away for the puppy to stay relaxed. Let them choose to approach — or not. Don’t lure them into strangers’ laps.  
- **Pair new things with good things.** Treats, play, or simply the option to move away.  
- **Sounds and surfaces:** low volume, optional approach, an escape route. **Never** flood them with loud firework recordings or “throw them in” approaches.  
- **Carry before they can walk out.** Before vaccinations allow walks in public, you can still carry them to watch traffic, people, and other dogs from a safe distance. Ask your vet what is safe where you live.  
- **Puppy class:** a small, reward-based class is well worth it. This book complements hands-on help; it does not replace it.

A simple list to think about, over the first few months — **gently, and only if the puppy is coping**:

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| People | Men, women, children (calm ones), people in hats, coats, glasses, uniforms, wheelchairs |
| Places | Pavement, car park, quiet café garden, a friend’s house, the vet’s waiting room |
| Sounds | Hoover, washing machine, doorbell, traffic, rain on the window |
| Surfaces | Grass, gravel, wet pavement, a wobbly cushion, a metal drain cover |
| Handling | Ears, paws, mouth, brushing, being lifted gently (Chapter 12) |
| Being alone | A few minutes behind a gate with a chew (Chapter 9) |
| Other animals | Calm, friendly adult dogs; cats at a distance; livestock from far away |

Do not try to tick everything off. The list is a reminder, not a quota. If exposure goes wrong — panic, shutdown — **stop**, simplify, and talk to your vet or a qualified trainer. Not louder socialisation.

### Feel first, then teach

Many popular puppy books — including Steve Mann’s *Easy Peasy Puppy Squeezy* — make the same point in plain language: **how your puppy feels matters more than what they do on the first try.** Safe, rested, and gently curious beats overwhelmed and pushed. That is **Training the Human** too: your hurry and your volume are part of their emotional weather.

### Start the basics early — gently

Puppies can learn the name game, sit, down, the hand touch, and settling on a mat from the first week or two at home. Keep sessions to a minute or two. Start time alone (Chapter 9) early, in tiny steps. And begin handling practice (Chapter 12) while they are small — a puppy who loves having their paws touched becomes an adult who is easy to care for.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Puppyhood is **short on sleep, long on mess**.  
- **Know:** Comparing yourself to adult dogs on Instagram is cruelty to yourself.  
- **Do:** Schedule toilet trips. Stock chews.  
- **Do:** Note one gentle new experience a week that went well.  
- **Don't:** Punish accidents indoors.  
- **Don't:** “Socialise” by handing the puppy to every stranger.  
- **Don't:** Blast scary sounds to “desensitise”.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The world is new. The bladder is small. Teeth are for exploring.  
- **Do:** Learn where toileting pays.  
- **Do:** Learn that soft mouths and toys get the good stuff.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that humans are unpredictable grabbers, or that fear must be endured.

## Section 2 — Teenagers: normal chaos, same method

Somewhere after the cute phase, many dogs **forget** everything you thought you had taught. Pulling gets stronger, recall wobbles, listening feels optional. This often starts somewhere around six months and can last well into the second year — longer in some large breeds. It is usually **normal development**, not proof you picked the wrong dog. It is also exhausting.

### What you might see

- Recall that was great now isn’t.  
- More pulling, more sniffing, more interest in other dogs.  
- New worries — things that never bothered them suddenly look scary.  
- More mouthing, chewing, or excitement.  
- Testing boundaries that seemed settled (the sofa, the kitchen counter).  
- Changes linked to hormones and, for some dogs, neutering decisions — a conversation to have with your vet.

### Your playbook

- **Shrink the world** again. Quiet walks, garden recall, easier asks (Chapters 5 and 6).  
- **Back to the long line** for recall (Chapter 10). Losing a teenager to a squirrel for twenty minutes undoes a lot of good work.  
- **Pay well.** They are not insulted by chicken. They are distracted by life.  
- **More prevention.** Gates, leads, things put out of reach. You are protecting all the good habits while their brain catches up.  
- **Enough rest and sniffing.** Teenagers can get overtired too. More exercise is not always the answer — calm, sniffy walks and naps often help more.  
- **Vet check** if the change is sudden or includes signs of pain. Adolescence is not an excuse to ignore health.  
- **Mantra on repeat:** “Make it easier — then ask again.”

Do not swap to harsh tools because the neighbour’s dog “respects” a shout. Teenage wobbles respond to **consistency and prevention**, not intimidation. Owners who stay patient now usually find a lovely adult dog waiting on the other side.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Adolescence is a **season**, not a verdict on your training.  
- **Do:** Lower the bar in public. Practise the hard bits in private.  
- **Do:** Celebrate small wins.  
- **Don't:** Add three new tricks because sit “stopped working”.  
- **Don't:** Train angry after work.  
- **Don't:** Give up on prevention when the world is too loud.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Brain and body are changing. Old habits need re-paving.  
- **Do:** Re-learn that check-ins and calm still pay, in boring places first.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that ignoring you is the only way to the fun, because you only practise in chaos.

## Section 3 — Rescue dogs: decompression, safety, help early

Rescue dogs arrive with **history you may not know**. Some settle in hours. Some take months. Day one is not “show them the city.”

### The first two weeks

**Decompression** means a quiet routine, predictable meals, lots of sleep, and very little that is new.

- **Keep the world small.** House, garden, short quiet walks — or no walks for the first day or two if the dog seems overwhelmed. A sniff in the garden is plenty.  
- **Same routine every day.** Meals, toilet trips, walks, and rest at the same times. Predictability helps a worried dog relax.  
- **No visitors.** The welcome party can wait. Tell friends you’ll introduce them in a few weeks.  
- **Let the dog choose.** Let them approach you rather than you reaching for them. Some rescues love a fuss on day one. Others need weeks before they enjoy touch.  
- **Safe places.** A bed in a quiet corner, maybe behind a gate. Nobody bothers them there (Chapter 9).  
- **Secure everything.** Rescue dogs are more likely to bolt in the first weeks. Check fences and gates. Use a secure harness, and consider a long line or double clip on walks. ID tag on from day one.  
- **Toilet training may need a refresher.** New house, new rules. Treat it like a puppy for a week or two.

### Collecting information, not judging

Assume nothing about other dogs, children, cats, being handled, or being left alone until you have seen it for yourself, safely. Keep a simple notebook: what made the dog worried, what made them relax, what they loved. In a few weeks, you will have a picture of who this dog really is.

**Growling is information.** Punish it at your peril (Chapter 7).

Some dogs seem perfect for the first few weeks, then show more of their personality — good and bad — as they relax. That is normal. It does not mean something has gone wrong. It means they feel at home enough to be themselves.

### Help early

Rescue centres often offer **post-adoption support** — ask. And **get help early** for repeated fear, aggression, or shutting down. Kindness is getting help before a crisis, not waiting until someone is bitten.

Match training to **readiness**, not your adoption timeline. Some dogs bloom in weeks. Some need months of quiet, prevention-first life. Both are valid. Start the basics — name, hand touch, settling — only when the dog is eating well, sleeping, and starting to explore.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** You are building **safety and predictability**, not a showcase dog.  
- **Do:** Keep a simple routine.  
- **Do:** Note triggers in a notebook.  
- **Do:** Call the rescue or a reward-based professional when stuck.  
- **Don't:** Host a welcome party.  
- **Don't:** Flood the dog with family visits.  
- **Don't:** Blame “abuse” for every behaviour without a vet and behaviour assessment.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The last home may have been scary. Your job is to prove this one has ways out.  
- **Do:** Learn the routine, and where rest is never disturbed.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that new people always mean being cornered, or that mistakes mean being shut away.

## Section 4 — A note on older dogs

Old dogs can learn new tricks. They may just need softer floors, shorter sessions, and longer naps.

- **Bodies change.** Stiff joints make sitting, lying down, stairs, and jumping into the car harder. If a dog stops doing something they used to, think comfort before “disobedience”. Non-slip rugs, ramps, and a softer bed help a lot.  
- **Senses change.** Hearing and eyesight can fade. Teach hand signals while hearing is good. Approach from the front and let them see or smell you before you touch them. A gentle foot-stamp can get a deaf dog’s attention.  
- **Minds change.** Some older dogs become confused, restless at night, or more anxious. That is a vet conversation — there is often help.  
- **Keep the brain busy.** Sniffing walks, food puzzles, and short, easy training games are good for older dogs too.  
- **Regular vet checks.** Older dogs benefit from more frequent check-ups. Many “behaviour changes” in older dogs turn out to be pain or illness.

## Practice lab — Pick one for your stage

### Puppy — gentle mouth (two minutes)

**You need:** a toy, small treats, a puppy who is awake but not frantic.

1. Stroke the puppy calmly for a few seconds.  
2. Soft mouth or no mouth? Mark and treat.  
3. Teeth on skin? Calmly offer the toy. Play with the toy.  
4. Stop after two minutes, or sooner if they are getting wound up.

**Stop if:** they are getting wilder, not calmer. They probably need a nap.

### Teenager — two good recalls and out

**You need:** the garden, jackpot treats.

1. Call once from a short distance, when nothing exciting is happening.  
2. Jackpot. Release.  
3. Once more. Then stop.

**Stop if:** they don’t come the first time. Make it easier tomorrow.

### Rescue — a decompression day

**You need:** a quiet day at home.

1. No visitors, no new places.  
2. Scatter breakfast on the grass or a snuffle mat. Let them sniff it out.  
3. A short, quiet walk or a garden potter. Chews in the afternoon.  
4. In your notebook, write down when they seemed relaxed and when they seemed worried.

**Look for:** longer naps, a softer body, maybe a first toy-play or a first lean against your legs.

## One method, four tempos

| Stage | First priority | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Puppy | Toilet, sleep, gentle new experiences | Flooding to “socialise” |
| Teenager | Shrink the world, re-pay the basics | New gadgets instead of prevention |
| Rescue | Decompression, safety, notes | A welcome party on day one |
| Older dog | Comfort, vet checks, easy brain games | Mistaking pain for disobedience |

Same loop, same five checks — a different patience setting on your dial.

## Troubleshooting

- **Puppy bites hard and won’t redirect** → Usually tired or over-excited. Nap time. Vet if there might be pain or teething trouble. Professional help if skin is breaking a lot.  
- **Puppy not getting toilet training** → More frequent trips, more supervision, bigger rewards outside. Vet if there are lots of accidents or straining.  
- **Teenager “won’t listen” only outside** → Place check. Better treats. Not a moral failure.  
- **Rescue regresses after a good week** → Normal. Reduce demands. Check health. Get help if fear or aggression grows.  
- **Older dog suddenly grumpy** → Vet first. Pain is the usual suspect.

## Stop if

- Biting that breaks skin, repeated aggression, or panic every day. Prevent and get help. Not DIY heroics.  
- You are rushing outings or visitors to “fix” fear. Stop. Distance and decompression.  
- The comfort check fails for days despite a quiet setup. Vet and qualified behaviour support.

## Before the next chapter

Chapter 14 gathers the **five checks** for when anything wobbles — setup, reward, ask, comfort, place — plus vet-first rules and how to find help in the UK’s unregulated training market. Bring your life-stage patience. Leave shame at the door.

Whichever stage you are in, the question is the same: *what is fair to ask today?* Puppies need sleep. Teenagers need easier asks. Rescues need time. Older dogs need comfort. The answer changes week to week. The method does not.
