## Opening

Recall is not a magic word. It is a **trust bank**. Every cheerful return you pay in is a deposit. Every grumpy grab is a withdrawal. Get it wrong and “come” becomes optional — or worse, something to avoid. Get it right in boring places first, and you earn the right to ask in harder ones **later**, with sensible safety (long line, secure space) and honest limits.

This chapter gives you **foundation recall**, a **long-line safety plan**, and **careful guidance** on distractions and off-lead judgement — grounded in Dogs Trust public guidance, not bravado.

You will need the **name game** from Chapter 8 first. A dog who turns when they hear their name has already done the first half of a recall.

**Bookmark this chapter** if **come when called** is your main goal. Good enough for most owners: reliable in the garden and on a long line, before you dream about empty beaches.

## Section 1 — Foundation recall

### Choose your word

Pick one recall cue and protect it. Many owners find “come” already has a bit of history — shouted crossly at the end of the park, or used to call the dog in for a bath. If so, choose a fresh word: “here”, “close”, “to me”, or a whistle. Agree it with everyone in the house.

Then make one promise: **this word always means good news.** Never use it to call the dog for something they dislike. If you need to trim nails, give a tablet, or leave the park, go and get the dog calmly instead.

### Teaching it

**One cheerful call**, one generous payoff. Indoors, or in a secure garden, say the recall cue **once** in a warm voice — not the voice you use when they have rolled in fox poo. When they arrive, **jackpot**: several small treats, one after another, plus praise. Then **release back to sniffing or play**. Coming to you must not mean “the fun ends.”

1. Start close. A metre or two away, when the dog is not busy with anything exciting.  
2. Say their name. When they turn, say your recall word once, and step backwards or crouch down. Moving away makes coming to you more fun.  
3. When they reach you, mark and give three to five treats, one at a time, close to your body.  
4. While they are eating, gently touch their collar or harness. Then give another treat. (More on this below.)  
5. Release them. “Free!” Let them go back to what they were doing.  
6. Five to eight goes. Stop at six if it is still crisp.

### The rules that protect the bank

- **Never punish a late return.** Dogs Trust is clear on this. If your dog takes ages and then finally comes, you still pay them. If you tell them off, they learn that coming back is bad news. If you only ever clip the lead on and march home, they learn that coming back ends the party.  
- **Do not repeat the cue** like a car alarm. Say it once. Wait. If they do not come, see “If they don’t come” below.  
- **Bank deposits in easy places** before you need withdrawals on the beach.

### Making the arrival lovely

Many dogs come *nearly* back, then dance just out of reach. That is usually because arriving has, in the past, meant being grabbed by the collar and taken somewhere boring. So teach them that hands near the collar are good news:

1. At home, gently touch the collar, then treat. Repeat a few times.  
2. Hold the collar for a second, then treat. Let go.  
3. Practise this in the middle of recall goes, as above.

Soon, a hand on the collar predicts cheese. Now catching your dog at the end of a walk is easy, kind, and drama-free.

### A game for two people: ping-pong recall

With a partner or friend, stand a few metres apart in the house or garden, each with treats. Take turns calling the dog, once each, and paying generously when they arrive. The dog runs back and forth, collecting jackpots. Keep it short and silly. It builds speed and joy into the word.

### If they don’t come

It will happen. Here is what to do instead of calling louder:

1. **Wait** — Dogs Trust suggests giving your dog about **five seconds** to respond before you try anything else. Sometimes they are finishing a sniff.  
2. **Make yourself interesting**: crouch down, clap softly, or run a few steps *away* from them, cheerfully. Most dogs chase movement.  
3. **Do not chase them.** That turns into the best game in the world, and they will be faster than you.  
4. If they still don’t come, **walk calmly to them**, pop the lead on, and give a treat anyway. Then make the next recall easier — closer, quieter, tastier.  
5. Make a note. You asked for something too hard in that place. That is information, not failure.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Recall fails when **your side** is inconsistent — sometimes jackpot, sometimes wrath.  
- **Do:** Carry treats on every outing, even when “they never need them”.  
- **Do:** Practise when nothing exciting is competing.  
- **Don't:** Call when you can’t follow through with something good.  
- **Don't:** Use recall to vent after they ignored you twice.  
- **Don't:** Play chase games where running away from you pays.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** “Come” predicts **arriving is worth it**, and often freedom again afterwards.  
- **Do:** Sprint towards you when the word is fresh and paid.  
- **Do:** Learn that checking in is not a trap.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that coming back means a telling-off, a jerk on the lead, or the end of every walk.

## Section 2 — Long lines, secure areas, and staying legal outdoors

Outdoors, **a long line is your friend**. A long line lets the dog move, sniff, and practise coming back without you dropping the lead on day one and hoping for the best.

### Long-line basics

- **Length:** something like five to ten metres is plenty for most owners. Longer lines are harder to handle.  
- **Attach to a harness, not a collar.** If the dog runs to the end of a long line on a collar, their neck takes the jolt.  
- **Material:** a flat, light line that doesn’t soak up water is easier to handle. Avoid thin cord that can cut your hands. Gloves help.  
- **Handling:** let the line run through your hand, then gather it up as the dog comes back. Keep it off the ground near your feet so nobody trips. Never wrap it round your hand or fingers.  
- **Slow down the stop:** if the dog heads off towards the end of the line, slow them gradually by closing your hand on the line, rather than letting it snap tight.  
- **Where:** never near roads, water, livestock, or cliffs where a tangle could be dangerous.

Practise handling the line at home or in an empty field before you need it. Clipping on, gathering up, and letting out are skills too.

### Secure spaces

Many areas now have **secure fields** you can hire by the hour — fenced spaces where your dog can run and practise without other dogs or roads. They are a brilliant halfway stage between garden and open country.

### Staying legal

In the UK, all dogs must be **microchipped and registered on an approved database by eight weeks old**. In a **public place** they must also **wear a collar and tag with your name and address** (see GOV.UK guidance). A chip is for when the collar comes off. It is not a substitute for teaching recall, and legal ID is not the same as “safe off lead everywhere.”

Local rules matter too: leads near livestock, on some beaches at certain times of year, in some parks. Check signs. The farmer’s sheep do not care how good your recall was last Tuesday.

### Plan before the gate opens

- Pay check-ins **before** you need a recall.  
- Know your exit: where you will go if the comfort check fails (Chapter 7).  
- Match line length to skill: shorter while learning.

If you cannot recall on a long line in a quiet field, you are not ready to think about off-lead in a squirrel arcade. That is not failure. It is honest criteria.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** The law and the land rules (livestock, beaches, parks) outrank your dog’s good day.  
- **Do:** Practise handling the line without an audience.  
- **Do:** End walks early while the bank still has credit.  
- **Don't:** Let the line go slack and then snap tight as a “correction”.  
- **Don't:** Assume a microchip replaces training.  
- **Don't:** Practise recall near traffic to “test it”.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** The line is a safety net, not a punishment.  
- **Do:** Get to practise recall with room to move. Get paid for coming back through grass and mud.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that freedom only happens when the line is off — so running away is the jackpot.

## Practice lab — Foundation recall, then long-line prep

Pick **one tier** per session. Do not do garden and field on the same day if either felt wobbly.

### Tier A — Indoor or secure garden (bank deposits)

**You need:** high-value treats, a dog who is not cornered.

1. Say the recall cue **once**. When they arrive, **jackpot** (three to five small treats) and praise.  
2. Touch the collar, treat.  
3. **Release** back to sniff or toy — the fun continues.  
4. Five to eight goes. Stop at six if still crisp.

**Stop if:** they avoid you, you repeat the cue, or you see signs of stress.

### Tier B — Quiet field on a long line (only when Tier A is boring)

**You need:** long line on a harness, jackpot treats, a quiet field, a plan for where you will leave if it gets busy.

1. Let them sniff on a slack line. Call **once** when they are not mid-sprint.  
2. Jackpot at your feet. Release to sniff again — the line stays on.  
3. Three to five goes. End while they still run towards you cheerfully.

**Stop if:** the line goes tight every time, they won’t eat, or you are calling to vent.

**Make it easier — then ask again:** shorter line, closer distance, or back to Tier A tomorrow. Section 3 adds distractions only when these tiers feel reliable.

## Section 3 — Distractions, long line, and off-lead judgement

Progress is **boring before it is brave**. You add distractions only when the trust bank is full in easier places — not when you need recall to work for the first time at the beach.

**Steps (careful):**

1. **Bank goes** in the garden and on quiet streets. Jackpot, release, never punish a late return.  
2. **Long line** outdoors, so the dog can move without learning “freedom = run away.” If they do not always come when called, Dogs Trust advises **not** relying on off-lead freedom. Use the line.  
3. **One distraction at a time** — a person in the distance, then a dog in the distance, then sniff-rich grass. Give time to respond (the five-second wait) and do not call again if it is unlikely to work.  
4. **Off-lead only when you are confident they will come back** in that particular place. Secure area first. Open country later. **Not** near roads, livestock, or cliffs.  
5. **ID** on, always.

### Calling away from fun

The hardest recall is away from something the dog loves — a game, a smell, another dog. Practise it on purpose, in easy versions:

- In the garden, toss a toy or a treat. Before they reach it, call. If they turn back, jackpot — **then send them to get the toy as well.** Coming to you did not cost them anything.  
- On the long line, call when they are sniffing something mildly interesting. Pay well, then say “go sniff” and let them go back.

This is called using the **thing they wanted as part of the reward**. It teaches the dog that coming back is not the end of fun. It is the way to more of it.

### When not to call

Sometimes the kindest, smartest thing is not to use your recall word at all. If you are pretty sure the dog won’t come — they are locked onto a squirrel, mid-greeting with another dog, or in full flight — calling just teaches them the word can be ignored. Instead, go and get them calmly, or use a known “this way!” turn (Chapter 8), or wait for a better moment. Save the recall word for when it is likely to work.

### An extra-special “emergency” recall (optional)

Some owners teach a second recall cue — a particular whistle, or an unusual word — that is **only ever** followed by an enormous jackpot: a whole handful of chicken, a squeaky toy party, the best thing in the world. Practised a few times a week at home and in the garden, and never used for anything dull, it can become very strong.

It is not a substitute for a long line or for good judgement. It is a backup for the day the gate gets left open.

### If recall falls apart

**Stop moving forward.** Shrink the world, go back to the long line, run the five checks (Chapter 14). Get reward-based professional help if escaping or chasing happens often.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Off-lead is a **privilege you earn**, not a right on day one.  
- **Do:** Write down where recall is “banked” and where it is “not yet”.  
- **Do:** Use the long line when you are unsure.  
- **Don't:** Drop the lead to prove a point.  
- **Don't:** Call repeatedly.  
- **Don't:** Punish returns.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Coming back must stay better than ignoring you.  
- **Do:** Learn that recall works in harder places, in small steps.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that off-lead means “never come back until the human is angry.”

## Section 4 — Chasing, escaping, and safety

Some dogs learn that **running away** is safer or more fun than staying close — because of previous homes, rough grabs, chase games you did not mean to teach, or simply a strong urge to chase. Breeds and types that were bred to hunt, herd, or run often find this especially hard.

If your dog bolts, fix **prevention** first:

- secure garden fences and gates (check for gaps, and things they could climb);  
- a lead or long line in open spaces until recall is genuinely reliable;  
- extra care at front doors and car boots (Chapter 12 covers doors).

Recall rebuilds with **choice** at short distances. Never chase as punishment.

If your dog chases livestock, wildlife, cyclists, or cars, treat it as a **serious safety issue**. Keep them on a lead or line in those places, and get help from a qualified professional (Chapter 14). In the UK, a dog worrying livestock can lead to serious consequences for dog and owner. More shouted cues will not fix a strong chase drive. A good plan, and sometimes a muzzle (trained kindly, with professional guidance), might.

## Troubleshooting

- **They come indoors but not in the garden** → Place check. Better treats outside. Shorter distance. Comfort check for outdoor worry.  
- **They run past you on arrival** → Pay **at** you, close to your body, not after you grab. Touch the collar, then treat.  
- **They come back, then dance out of reach** → Practise the collar-touch game at home. Stop using arrival to end fun.  
- **You are angry when they return** → Stop calling for today. Rebuild with easy goes. Never punish a late return.  
- **Recall was great, now it’s gone** → Often adolescence (Chapter 13). Back to the long line. Shrink the world. It will come back.

## Stop if

- You are near roads, cliffs, water, or livestock without a lead, line, or fence. **Stop** asking. Use the lead.  
- Growling, avoiding you, or shutting down when you call. Comfort failed. Simplify. Do not chase.  
- You are calling to prove a point or to vent. **Stop.** Walk home in silence. Train tomorrow.
