## Opening

A walk is not a treadmill. It is sniffing, pacing, weather, and the occasional pigeon that was definitely a dragon. **Walking on a lead** should be survivable for both of you: equipment that does not hurt, a dog who is happy to wear the harness, and short stretches where checking in pays — not a daily tug-of-war judged by strangers at the bus stop.

**Bookmark this chapter** for **walk nicely** — one of the three basics from Chapter 1. Remember the **two kinds of walk**: **leisure** (sniff, be a dog) and **training** (short, one goal). Loose-lead teaching lives here. Do not lead-police a leisure walk and call it failure.

This chapter covers **why dogs pull**, **comfortable kit**, **getting used to lead and harness**, **loose-lead mechanics** (from Dogs Trust public guidance), **leaving the house calmly**, and **real walks** with good sense about where and when to stop.

## Section 1 — Why dogs pull (it isn’t personal)

Before you fix pulling, it helps to stop taking it personally.

- **Dogs are faster than us.** Our normal walking pace is a slow dawdle to most dogs. Walking beside a human is a strange, unnatural speed for them.  
- **The world is exciting.** Smells, dogs, people, the park gate. Of course they want to get there.  
- **Pulling works.** Every time a tight lead has moved you forwards — even one step — pulling has been paid. Most dogs have been paid thousands of times.  
- **Equipment and comfort.** A badly fitting harness, sore joints, or a worried dog trying to get away from something can all look like “pulling”.

None of that is the dog being difficult. It is just what has happened so far. The fix is to change what pays: **slack lead gets you where you want to go; tight lead doesn’t.** That takes time, and patience on your side of the lead.

## Section 2 — Equipment and comfort (no pain kit)

**Fit first.** A harness or collar should allow two fingers under the straps, sit clear of the armpits, and not rub. If the dog freezes, scratches, or rolls when you put it on, suspect fit or bad associations — not “stubbornness.”

**No pain kit in this book.** Prong collars, choke chains, shock devices, spray collars, and “corrections” that rely on fear are out. UK welfare guidance favours reward-based teaching. Your walk should not depend on something that makes the dog flinch when you reach for the cupboard.

### Choosing a harness: the Y shape and two rings

You will hear “get a Y harness” a lot. Helpful — if you know what to look for. A **Y shape** means a strap runs down between the shoulders, rather than a tight band straight across the chest that restricts the front legs. You also want **two metal rings (O-rings)**: one on the **back** and one on the **front** of the chest. Many harnesses only have a back ring. For this book, **both** matter.

- **Training walk:** clip to the **front** ring for early loose-lead work. If the dog surges ahead, the lead tends to turn them gently back towards you, instead of towing you from behind like a sledge dog. That is a kindness to your shoulders while you teach.  
- **Leisure walk:** the **back** ring is usually fine once check-ins are a habit, or on any day you are not training.

Same harness, different ring — matching the two kinds of walk. Some owners use a double-ended lead clipped to both rings, which gives a little extra steering. If your dog has an unusual shape or the straps rub, a good shop fitter or vet nurse beats guessing in the pet aisle.

### Collars, leads, and the rest

- **Collars:** some dogs walk fine on a flat collar. Many are more comfortable in a harness, especially dogs who pull, small dogs, and dogs with breathing or neck problems. Your dog still needs a collar and ID tag in public (Chapter 10).  
- **Leads:** a normal lead of about one and a half to two metres gives the dog room to sniff without tangling you up.  
- **Extending (retractable) leads:** these teach the dog that pulling makes the lead longer — the opposite of what you want. They can also cause injuries if the cord wraps around fingers or legs. Fine for some leisure situations with a dog who already walks nicely; not for teaching.  
- **Head collars:** some owners use them for very strong dogs. They need slow, careful introduction (Section 3), and many dogs find them uncomfortable. If you are considering one, get advice from a qualified reward-based trainer first.

**Hands:** hold the lead so a sudden lunge won’t burn your palm. Never wrap it round your fingers or wrist.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Uncomfortable kit can look like “bad on the lead”. Check fit before months of “training.”  
- **Do:** Let the dog wear gear indoors for seconds, then minutes, before the street.  
- **Do:** Check the fit after puppy growth spurts.  
- **Don't:** Buy a “Y harness” with only a back ring and wonder why no one mentioned the front.  
- **Don't:** Buy the tightest harness “so they feel it”.  
- **Don't:** Yank to teach.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Harness and lead predict outings. They should not predict a squeezed throat or panic.  
- **Do:** Link gear with food, play, and short happy trips.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that being caught means pain or a chase.

## Section 3 — Getting used to lead and harness

**Good feelings first** beats “clip on and hope.” At home:

1. Show the harness. Mark and treat for sniffing it or coming over.  
2. Touch the straps to the dog’s body briefly. Treat. Take it away before they fuss.  
3. Hold the head loop open and lure their head through with a treat. Treat. Take it off. Many dogs dislike things going over their head, so go slowly here.  
4. Do up one buckle. Undo it. Treat. Build up to wearing the whole thing while they eat a scatter of treats from the floor.  
5. Clip the lead on and let it trail indoors for a minute (supervised). Then pick it up and hold it slack while they walk towards a treat you have tossed.

One change at a time. If they buck or bite the lead, pause. Chewing the lead is common in young dogs. Try shorter goes, a toy to carry, or a chew for the mouth — not wrestling.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** This is about feelings, not walking. Patience here saves months of pavement war.  
- **Do:** Keep goes under a minute.  
- **Do:** Stop before they try to wriggle out of the harness.  
- **Don't:** Drag them out of the door to “get used to it”.  
- **Don't:** Practise when you are already late for work.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Clips and straps are just objects that predict snacks and the garden.  
- **Do:** Stand still for gear-up. Move with you when the lead is slack in easy indoor games.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that fighting the harness ends the session.

## Section 4 — The walk starts at the front door

Many walks are lost before they begin. The dog sees the lead, spins, barks, bounces off the walls, and explodes through the front door — and that is the energy they bring to the pavement.

Make leaving calmer:

1. **Pick up the lead and wait.** Do nothing until the dog is calmer. Sit down if you need to. You are teaching that bouncing doesn’t make the walk happen.  
2. **Lead on when they are still.** Any calm moment — four paws down, or a sit. If they leap up, pause. Try again.  
3. **Door handle test.** Put your hand on the handle. If the dog rushes forwards, take your hand away. When they hang back or look at you, open the door a crack. Rush again? Close it gently. Calm? Open it more.  
4. **You go through, then invite them.** Or they go through calmly on a release word. Either is fine. What matters is that the doorway is not a launch pad.  
5. **Pause outside.** A few seconds to sniff the step and look around before you set off.

It might take ten minutes the first time. It gets quicker. And a dog who leaves the house calmly usually walks better for the first few minutes, which is when pulling habits are strongest. Chapter 12 has more about doors.

## Section 5 — Soft-lead mechanics

This method follows Dogs Trust’s guidance on teaching a dog to walk nicely on a lead. Use a comfortable harness or flat collar. No pain-based “anti-pull” kit.

**Build in order:**

1. **Pay at your side** indoors or in the garden — calm places first. Pick a side (left or right — it doesn’t matter, but stick to one while learning). Deliver treats right by your leg, at the dog’s nose height. That spot becomes the best place in the world.  
2. **Add movement.** Take a step. If the dog is still by your side, treat there. Then two steps. Slowly stretch the time and distance between treats.  
3. **Change direction** now and then, cheerfully, so they learn to keep an eye on you. Pay when they catch up.  
4. **The core rule: loose lead = forward.** The instant the lead goes tight, **stop**. Stand still and quiet. Do not jerk or scold. Move again only when the lead goes slack — when the dog steps back, turns, or looks at you. If they don’t, take a few steps **the other way** to get their attention back, then try again.

Expect walks to take much longer while you are learning. That is why training walks are short.

### Making it work in real life

- **Treat often at first.** Every few steps on a quiet street is not too many. You can spread treats out later.  
- **Let sniffing be a reward.** After a nice loose stretch, say “go sniff” and let them explore a lamppost. Sniffing is a brilliant reward that costs you nothing.  
- **Vary your pace.** Speeding up a little often helps dogs stay with you. Slow, plodding walks can be harder.  
- **Short is good.** Five minutes of real loose-lead practice, then a leisure walk, beats forty minutes of stop-start misery.

**If you truly cannot be consistent today** (late for work, injury, chaos at home): Dogs Trust mentions using two different harnesses — one for days when pulling is allowed, one for training walks. This book treats that as a **last resort**, not a lifestyle. Dogs learn slowly when the rules change with the wardrobe. Prefer a **shorter leisure walk** on the usual kit, or skip training and try tomorrow.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Pulling has worked before. Every step forward on a tight lead taught them that. Patience is the method.  
- **Do:** Stop the instant you feel tension.  
- **Do:** Pay at your side. Quit while it’s calm.  
- **Don't:** Jerk, shout, or march on through a tight lead because people are watching.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Tight lead = pause. Slack lead = explore with you.  
- **Do:** Check in at your side for a treat.  
- **Do:** Learn that direction changes predict treats.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that pulling is the only way to reach the smell.

*Note:* some trainers teach loose-lead walking without stopping. This book follows **Dogs Trust public guidance**. Avoid jerking the lead, and avoid kit that works through pain or startle — if a method needs that, it is not in this book’s kit.

## Section 6 — Real walks: environment, check-ins, ending early

When gear is boring at home, take **short practice stretches** outside. Pick quiet times first. Pay attention **to you** sometimes — the name game from Chapter 8, a treat at your side — then release to sniff. Sniffing is not disobedience. It is dog email.

On a **training walk**, pick **one goal** for that outing. For example: “loose-ish lead for one short street” **or** “three check-ins before the park gate.” Not both, plus perfect position, plus no squirrels.

### Meeting things on the walk

Real walks have dogs, people, bikes, cats, and bins. Some dogs lunge, bark, or freeze. Give yourself and your dog space:

- **Cross the road, step into a driveway, or turn around** (“this way!” from Chapter 8) **before** your dog reacts, not after.  
- **Feed your dog** as the other dog or person passes, at a distance where they can still eat.  
- **Don’t let on-lead greetings happen by default.** Dog-to-dog meetings on tight leads often go wrong. Ask first. It is fine to say “sorry, he’s in training” and keep walking.  
- **Choose quieter routes** while you are learning. There is no prize for practising on the busiest street.

If your dog regularly lunges, barks, or growls at dogs or people, that is more than a loose-lead problem. Go back to Chapter 7, keep more distance, and get help from a qualified professional (Chapter 14).

### End early

**Comfort check** every walk: stiff body, won’t eat, scanning and unable to settle — shorten the route, or go home. **End early** while the lead still feels like a partnership. Tomorrow’s walk matters more than today’s pride.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Passers-by are not scoring you. Ending early is skilled, not embarrassing.  
- **Do:** Plan turn-back points.  
- **Do:** Carry treats. Note which streets get your dog wound up.  
- **Don't:** March on until everyone is miserable.  
- **Don't:** Practise only at rush hour because “they must learn.”

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Every street is different — new smells, new rules.  
- **Do:** Learn that checking in sometimes pays, and that sniffing is part of the deal.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that pulling is the only way to the good bit because slack never gets there.

## Practice lab — Gear-up, then stop-start on a quiet street

Two parts, in one week — not both in one grumpy hour.

### Part 1 — Gear-up (indoors)

**You need:** harness, lead, small treats.

1. Harness on, then five treats scattered on the floor or five mark-and-pays for standing still. Harness off while they are still happy.  
2. Add the lead. Hold it slack. Dog moves one step towards a treat. Pay. Unclip.

**Stop if:** they bite the gear, freeze, or you are late for work. Tomorrow counts.

### Part 2 — Stop-start on a quiet street (when Part 1 is boring)

**You need:** the same harness, **front ring** for training, treats in your pocket, a quiet street at a quiet time.

1. Leave the house calmly (Section 4).  
2. **Loose lead = forward.** The instant you feel tension, **stop** — quiet feet, no jerk.  
3. When slack returns, either step forward or give a treat at your side — your choice today, not both.  
4. Three to six stop-start cycles. Then **turn for home** while the lead still feels manageable. Let the way home be a leisure walk.

**Look for:** a dog who starts to glance back at you, or slows down by themselves before the lead tightens.

**If it’s not working:** shorter route, more treats at your side before moving, or Part 1 only this week. **Make it easier — then ask again.**

**Stop if:** the lead stays tight every single time, the dog won’t eat, or you shout. End the walk. No lesson is worth that.

## Weather, kit, and realistic walks

British walks include rain, mud, and dark afternoons. Adjust your expectations: a wet dog may be less interested in food; a cold day may need a shorter route. A towel by the door beats nagging on the mat. High-vis for you, and lights or reflective gear for the dog, are sensible kit — not training failures when you skip a stormy rush-hour pavement.

On hot days, walk early or late, carry water, and keep it short. Pavements can burn paws — if it is too hot for the back of your hand, it is too hot for their feet. Flat-faced breeds and older dogs struggle much sooner in heat.

## Troubleshooting

- **They plant and won’t move** → Comfort check. Try somewhere easier. Vet if this is new. Do not drag.  
- **They lunge at dogs or people** → More distance and prevention (cross the road, turn away). This is not just a loose-lead problem. Get help if it is frequent.  
- **They pull worst at the start of the walk** → Calm exit (Section 4). Have a sniff break at the end of your road. Start training a few minutes into the walk, not at the gate.  
- **They walk nicely at home but not outside** → Normal. Place again. Shrink the world: driveway, quiet street, quiet time.  
- **Your hands or shoulder hurt** → Front-ring harness, shorter walks, and professional help for very strong pulling.

## Stop if

- Growling, snapping, or air-snapping on the lead. Make space. See Chapter 14.  
- The dog flinches from the harness or lead. Stop. Check fit and pain.  
- You are jerking or shouting. **Stop.** No lesson is worth that.

## Before the next chapter

Chapter 12 handles **doors and greetings** — another flashpoint for excitement. A dog who can check in on a quiet street is better prepared for “someone’s at the door.”

Measure progress in **check-ins, stop-start wins, and comfort**, not in metres without tension. A walk that ends early with one loose moment and a treat is a better training walk than a grim lap counted as “exercise.”
