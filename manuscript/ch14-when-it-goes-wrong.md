## Opening

You will have days when the treat pouch feels pointless, the recall fails in front of witnesses, and you wonder if you are the only person whose dog “doesn’t get it.” You are not. Dogs are living animals. Life stages, pain, and pavement chaos all get in the way. This chapter is the **pause button** from Chapter 1: troubleshoot without blame, rule out health, and know when this book must step aside for professionals.

Most owners only wanted **sit, come, walk nicely** — and hit a wall anyway. That is normal. **Bookmark this chapter** for when you are stuck or grumpy. You are not failing for needing it.

## Section 1 — Troubleshooting the loop and the five checks

When a skill stalls, run the **teaching loop** in your head: setup → cue → behaviour → mark and reward → release → reset. Something in that chain broke. Before you repeat the cue louder, use the **five checks** and the owner mantra:

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

### The five checks (full table)

Cut this out, photograph it, or stick it on the fridge.

| Plain words | Check | Ask yourself | Typical fixes | Stop or get help if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Place** | Setup | Is it calm enough for *this* dog *today*? | Move indoors. Quieter street. Gate or lead. | You cannot make a safe setup at home |
| **Pay** | Reward | Do they want the treat or toy **right now**? | Better food. Play. Train before meals. | Won’t take food even in easy places — vet / behaviour help |
| **Ask** | Criterion | **One** clear ask, not three at once? | Smaller ask. One goal per walk. | You keep adding asks because you are embarrassed |
| **Mood** | Comfort | Signs of stress (Chapter 7)? Won’t eat, stiff, trying to leave? | Stop teaching. Simpler place. More distance. | Growling, biting, or shutting down most days |
| **Same at home?** | Place change | Worked indoors but not on the pavement? | **Easier place** — house → garden → outside | Sudden failure everywhere — vet first |

**Easier place** (the same idea as “shrink the world”) means practise where the dog can succeed again, then grow in small steps — not one heroic outing to “prove” progress.

Optional habit: when **you** fix the plan instead of blaming the dog, a quiet **“Good human.”** — a marker for the trainer in the room.

### Worked examples

**Pavement recall.** It worked in the garden yesterday. Today your dog sniffs the air and wanders off while a spaniel owner gives you a look. **Same at home?** failed first — new smells, other dogs in view, and you rushed out after a meeting. **Mood** — they are stiff and won’t eat. **Stop** calling. Turn for home on the lead. Tomorrow, practise five easy recalls on the long line in a quieter field before you ask on the street again. No lecture for either of you. The spaniel’s Instagram is not your report card.

**Mat settle.** The dog lies down but pops up every ten seconds. **Ask** — you wanted too long, too soon. **Pay** — kibble may be boring after dinner. Pay for shorter calm. Train before tea. Release often. If they refuse all food on the mat in an empty room, that is **Mood** — and possibly the vet — not more “calm down.”

**Down that vanished.** Down was perfect in the lounge. On the kitchen tiles, nothing. **Place** — tiles are cold and slippery; many dogs don’t want to lie on them. Bring the mat. **Ask** — you were holding a shopping bag. Put it down. Lure once, mark, treat, free. *Good human.*

**Leaving for work.** The dog was fine for an hour last week. Today the neighbour says they howled all morning. **Mood** — check the stress bucket: was there a storm, a vet visit, building work? **Ask** — three hours was a big jump from one. Go back to shorter absences, arrange a dog walker for longer days, and film the next practice. If the howling continues, Chapter 9’s “when to get help” applies.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Stalls are usually **plan problems**, not character flaws in either species.  
- **Do:** Write down which check failed.  
- **Do:** Change one thing. End on a small win or a clean stop.  
- **Don't:** Drill until both of you hate the skill.  
- **Don't:** Skip the comfort check because “we’re already out”.  
- **Don't:** Treat getting help as defeat.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They need predictable payoffs and a way out of pressure.  
- **Do:** Get easier goes after a failure.  
- **Do:** Trust that you will notice stress.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that cues mean “ignore until the human shouts.”

## Section 2 — Common problems, quick guide

A one-stop list of everyday problems, what usually helps, and when it is time for help. It is a starting point, not a treatment plan.

| Problem | Usually helps | Where | Get help if… |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Jumping up** | Prevent (lead, gate). Pay four paws on the floor. Treats scattered low. | Chapters 2, 12 | It comes with growling or nipping |
| **Pulling on the lead** | Comfortable harness, front ring. Stop when tight, go when slack. Short training walks. | Chapter 11 | You can’t hold the dog safely |
| **Not coming back** | Long line. Fresh recall word. Jackpots. Never punish a late return. | Chapters 8, 10 | They chase livestock, cars, or run off for long periods |
| **Barking at the window or door** | Block the view. Pay quiet. “Thank you” and call away. | Chapters 9, 12 | It is constant, or the dog seems frightened |
| **Mouthing and nipping** | Toys to bite. Pay soft mouths. Naps. | Chapter 13 | Skin breaks often, or it happens when the dog is worried rather than playing |
| **Chewing things** | Put things away. Plenty of legal chews. Enough exercise and sniffing. | Chapters 9, 13 | It only happens when alone — could be distress |
| **Toilet accidents** | More trips out. Pay outside. Clean with enzyme cleaner. No telling off. | Chapter 13 | Sudden change in an adult dog — vet |
| **Distress when left** | Don’t leave them longer than they can cope with. Start again very slowly. | Chapter 9 | Always, if the dog is already distressed — vet and behaviourist |
| **Guarding food, toys, or beds** (stiffening, growling, snapping if you come near) | Don’t take things away. Give space while eating. Swap for something better rather than grabbing. Prevent situations with children. | Chapter 7 | Any growling or snapping over things — get help early |
| **Fear of noises (fireworks, thunder)** | A safe den, curtains closed, TV or radio on, walk earlier in the day. Stay calm and comfort them if they want it. | Chapter 7 | Every year it is bad — talk to your vet well before the season |
| **Lunging or barking at other dogs or people on walks** | Distance. Cross the road. U-turn. Feed as they pass at a distance. | Chapters 7, 11 | It is frequent, getting worse, or anyone could be hurt |

Comforting a frightened dog does not “reward the fear.” Fear is a feeling, not a trick. If your dog wants to be close to you during fireworks, let them.

## Section 3 — Vet first

Training assumes a body that feels well enough to learn. **See the vet** when you notice:

- **Pain or illness** — limping, flinching when touched, licking one spot, tummy upsets, coughing, changes in eating or drinking.  
- **Sudden behaviour change** — a friendly dog snaps; a confident dog freezes; sleep or appetite shifts.  
- **New toilet accidents** in a dog who was clean.  
- **Before** you decide fear or aggression is “just training”.

Your vet may refer you on to a behaviourist. Ruling out medical causes is not optional drama. UK welfare guidance treats health as part of responsible ownership.

Bring notes and, if you can do it safely, a short video. Behaviour that happens at home often vanishes in the consulting room.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “He’s fine at the vet” once a year is not a behaviour consultation.  
- **Do:** Book a check when a change is sudden.  
- **Do:** Bring notes and video.  
- **Don't:** Increase exercise or corrections to “burn off” unexplained aggression.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Pain makes every cue feel like a threat.  
- **Do:** Get relief, so learning can restart.  
- **Don't:** Be trained through untreated discomfort.

## Section 4 — Trainers vs behaviourists (UK signposts, not endorsements)

The UK dog training market is **largely unregulated**. Anyone may call themselves a trainer or a behaviourist. That puts the job on **you** to ask questions.

### Who does what

- **Dog trainers** teach skills: recall, lead walking, manners, puppy classes. Great for everyday training problems.  
- **Clinical animal behaviourists** work with fear, aggression, separation distress, and compulsive behaviours. They usually work on referral from your vet, and look at health, history, and environment together.  
- **Veterinary behaviourists** are vets with extra qualifications in behaviour, and can prescribe medication where it is needed.

As a rough guide: **skills problem → trainer. Emotional or safety problem → vet, then clinical behaviourist.**

### What to look for

- **Methods:** reward-based. No pain, fear, or intimidation. Walk away if not.  
- **Qualifications:** clear about their training and experience. Membership of a recognised body is **one** good sign, not a guarantee.  
- **Scope:** knows their limits, and refers on to vets and clinical behaviourists when needed.

**Public signposts** (check current listings):

- [ABTC (Animal Behaviour and Training Council)](https://www.abtc.org.uk/) — keeps registers of trainers and clinical animal behaviourists who meet agreed standards.  
- [IMDT (Institute of Modern Dog Trainers)](https://www.imdt.uk.com/) — a force-free training community with a referral culture when cases go beyond everyday training.  
- **Your vet** — often the best first call, and can refer you on.  
- **Dogs Trust** runs a free behaviour support line for owners — check their website for current details.

This book does not endorse individuals. It endorses **asking**, **walking away from aversive methods**, and **getting help early**.

### Questions to ask on the phone (politely)

- What will we do in the first session if my dog is frightened?  
- Do you use prong collars, shock collars, spray collars, or “corrections” that startle or hurt?  
- When do you refer to a vet or a clinical behaviourist?  
- What do you do if my dog stiffens or growls?  
- What qualifications and insurance do you have?

Clear answers beat vague charisma. You are hiring help for a family member, not auditioning for a reality show.

### Classes

A good class is small, calm, and kind. Watch one before you sign up if you can. Good signs: dogs and owners look relaxed, there is space between dogs, the trainer uses treats and toys, and owners are allowed to take a break or leave. Warning signs: lots of jerking leads, shouting, dogs looking frightened, or a trainer who tells you your dog is “dominant”.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A charismatic social media account is not a qualification.  
- **Do:** Interview professionals. Ask for a plain-language plan and stop rules.  
- **Don't:** Hire someone who blames dominance for everything.  
- **Don't:** Let anyone “fix” aggression in one session on your kitchen floor.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They deserve methods that do not trade fear for obedience.  
- **Do:** Work with humans who read stress signs and keep everyone safe.  
- **Don't:** Be put through flooding or punishment sold as “balance”.

## Section 5 — What this book never replaces

This guide teaches **everyday reward-based skills**, reading stress, and honest stop rules. It does **not** replace:

- veterinary diagnosis or treatment;  
- behaviour therapy for serious aggression, severe anxiety, separation distress, or welfare crises;  
- legal advice (dangerous dogs law, incidents, housing);  
- plans for multi-dog households beyond the basics below.

**Multi-dog homes:** prevention — separate feeding, supervised introductions, ways out for each dog — stops conflict being practised. Train one dog at a time while the other has a chew behind a gate. If dogs in the home fight, separate them and get professional help. Training one dog while the other rehearses chaos through a gate is a setup problem, not a moral test.

If you use a companion app, treat it as **practice prompts**, not a clinician. When the app and the book disagree with your dog’s safety, **the dog wins** — simplify, prevent, call for help.

## When guilt shows up

Many owners delay the vet or the behaviourist because they fear judgement — from the receptionist, the trainer, or themselves. This book’s position: **asking early is competent**, not weak. A dog who growls at the groomer is communicating. A teenager who pulls is developmentally noisy. A rescue who hides is not “ungrateful.” Guilt is a poor training plan. Use the table, use the mantra, use the phone.

**Red flags that skip the table and go straight to help:** bites that cause injury; a dog who cannot eat or rest for long periods; you are afraid of your own dog; children are at risk; dogs in the home are fighting; a dog hurting themselves. Safety first — separating, muzzle training with professional guidance, an emergency vet — comes before another chapter.

## Decision sketch (not a flowchart tattoo)

1. **Safe right now?** If not → separate, secure, vet or emergency vet as needed.  
2. **Sudden or painful?** → Vet.  
3. **Teaching stall only?** → Five checks, then an easier go.  
4. **Fear or aggression pattern?** → Reward-based professional, with the vet involved.  
5. **Still stuck?** → Second opinion. You are allowed to sack a trainer who frightens your dog.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Knowing when to stop reading and start dialling is **success**.  
- **Do:** Keep your vet’s number and one professional contact somewhere easy to find.  
- **Don't:** Delay help because you wanted to finish the book first.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They cannot read the chapter list. If they are suffering, they need relief now.  
- **Do:** Benefit from qualified help when problems go beyond kitchen-scale teaching.  
- **Don't:** Wait until somebody is hurt to admit it is beyond you.

## Troubleshooting

- **You can’t tell which check failed** → Assume mood and place. Make the setup easier tomorrow. Change one thing.  
- **Professionals disagree** → Choose methods you can live with ethically. Get a second opinion. Use the vet as the hub.  
- **You can’t afford help** → Some charities offer free advice lines; group classes cost less than one-to-ones. Prevention is free — distance, gates, leads.  
- **Family members disagree about methods** → Show them the deal from Chapter 1. Agree the word list and the “no pain, no fear” rule. A professional can help settle it.

## Stop if

- Any bite breaking skin, a child at risk, or a dog who cannot rest. **Stop** DIY training. Make everyone safe. Vet and professional.  
- You are following advice that frightens the dog. **Stop**, even if you paid for it.  
- You are using training to vent anger. **Stop.** Walk away. Try another day, or get another human to help you.

## A last word

There is no Chapter 15 — only the walk you take tomorrow with a clearer plan.

You started this book wanting a dog who sits, comes back, and walks without pulling your arm off. Some of that will have happened. Some of it is still in progress, and some of it may look different from what you imagined. That is fine. The deal was never perfection. It was clarity, kindness, honesty, and the freedom to stop.

If you remember only three things, make them these:

1. **Pay for what you like.** The dog will do more of it.  
2. **Prevent what you don’t.** Gates and leads are kindness, not failure.  
3. **When it goes wrong, make it easier — then ask again.**

Re-read the five checks when pride whispers to push. Ask for help early. Stop while it is still fun.

Good dog. *Good human.*
