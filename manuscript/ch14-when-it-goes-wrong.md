## Opening

You will have days when the treat pouch feels pointless, the recall fails in front of witnesses, and you wonder if you are the only person whose dog “doesn’t get it.” You are not. Dogs are living animals; life stages, pain, and pavement chaos all interfere. This chapter is the **systematic pause** promised since Chapter 1: troubleshoot without blame, rule out health, and know when this book — and any app beside it — must step aside for professionals.

## Section 1 — Troubleshooting the loop and the five checks

When a skill stalls, run the **teaching loop** in your head: setup → cue → behaviour → mark/reward → release → reset. Something in that chain broke. Before you repeat the cue louder, use the **five checks** and the owner mantra:

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

### The five checks (full table)

| Check | Ask yourself | Typical fixes | Stop / refer if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Setup** | Is the environment small and calm enough for *this* dog *today*? | Move indoors; quieter street; fewer people; use gate or lead | You cannot create a safe setup (e.g. unavoidable aggression in home) |
| **2. Reward** | Does this dog want what you are offering **right now**? | Higher value food; play; distance from scary thing as “reward”; train before meals | Refuses all rewards repeatedly in easy contexts — vet / behaviour |
| **3. Criterion** | Are you asking for **one** clear thing, not duration + distance + distraction at once? | Pick one dimension; shorten sit; accept check-in instead of heel | You keep adding criteria because you are embarrassed — pause, regroup |
| **4. Comfort** | Any **stress cluster** (Chapter 12)? Panting, stiff, won’t eat, fleeing, tight bark? | Stop teaching; simplify; manage; increase distance | Growling, snapping, biting, shutdown most days |
| **5. Context** | Did it work at home but fail on the pavement? | **Shrink the world** — practise where success is likely; grow slowly | Sudden failure everywhere — vet first |

**Shrink the world** is plain English for lowering difficulty and distraction until the dog can succeed again, then generalising in modest steps — not a single heroic outing to “prove” progress.

Optional habit: when **you** fix the plan instead of blaming the dog, a quiet **“Good human.”** — marker for the trainer in the room.

**Worked example (pavement recall):** It worked in the garden yesterday; today your dog sniffs the cue and wanders off. Check 5 — context — failed first: new smells, other dogs visible, you rushed out after a meeting. Check 4 — they are stiff and will not eat. **Stop** calling. Turn for home on the lead; tomorrow practise five easy recalls on the long line in a quieter field before you ask on the street again. No moral lecture for either of you.

**Worked example (mat settle):** Dog lies down but pops up every ten seconds. Check 3 — criterion — is too long too soon; Check 2 — kibble may be weak after dinner. Pay for shorter stillness; train before tea; release often. If they refuse all food on the mat in an empty room, Check 4 and vet — not more “calm down.”

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Stalls are usually **plan problems**, not character flaws in either species.  
- **Do:** Write which check failed; change one variable; end session on a small win or a clean stop.  
- **Don't:** Drill until both of you hate the skill; skip comfort because “we’re already out”; treat referral as defeat.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They need predictable payoffs and escape from pressure.  
- **Do:** Experience easier reps after failure; trust that you will notice stress.  
- **Don't:** Learn that your name or cue means “ignore until human shouts.”

## Section 2 — Vet first

Training assumes a body that feels well enough to learn. **See the vet** when:

- **Pain or illness** — limping, flinching on touch, excessive licking, gut upset, coughing.  
- **Sudden behaviour change** — friendly dog snaps; confident dog freezes; appetite or sleep shifts.  
- **Before** you label fear or aggression as “training only.”

Your vet may refer to a behaviourist; medical rule-out is not optional drama. RSPCA-aligned welfare thinking treats health as part of responsible ownership (`claim-rspca-003`, `claim-rspca-004` — paraphrase in register).

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “He’s fine at the vet” once a year is not a behaviour consultation.  
- **Do:** Book a check when change is sudden; bring notes and video (safely).  
- **Don't:** Increase exercise or corrections to “burn off” unexplained aggression.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Pain makes every cue feel like a threat.  
- **Do:** Get relief so learning can restart.  
- **Don't:** Be trained through untreated discomfort.

## Section 3 — Trainers vs behaviourists (UK signposts, not endorsements)

The UK dog training market is **largely unregulated** — anyone may call themselves a trainer or behaviourist (`claim-abtc-001-unregulated`). That puts the burden on **you** to ask questions:

- Methods: reward-based, no pain/fear/intimidation? Walk away if not.  
- Qualifications: transparent training; membership of recognised bodies as **one** signal, not a guarantee.  
- Scope: trainers teach skills; **clinical** fear, aggression, and compulsive patterns often need a **veterinary behaviourist** or certified clinical behaviourist working with your vet.

**Public signposts** (verify current listings at publication):

- [ABTC](https://www.abtc.org.uk/) — registers practitioners to agreed standards.  
- [IMDT](https://www.imdt.uk.com/) — force-free training community; referral culture when cases exceed owner scope (`claim-imdt-003-referral`).

This book does not endorse individuals. It endorses **asking**, **walking away from aversives**, and **referring early**.

**Questions to ask on the phone (politely):**

- What will we do in the first session if my dog is frightened?  
- Do you use prong, shock, or “corrections” that startle or hurt?  
- When do you refer to a vet or clinical behaviourist?  
- Can you describe stop rules if my dog stiffens or growls?

Clear answers beat vague charisma. You are hiring help for a family member, not auditioning for a reality show.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** A charismatic social media account is not a credential.  
- **Do:** Interview professionals; ask for plain-language plan and stop rules.  
- **Don't:** Hire someone who blames dominance for everything; let anyone “fix” aggression in one session on your kitchen floor.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They deserve methods that do not trade fear for compliance.  
- **Do:** Work with humans who read stress clusters and manage safety.  
- **Don't:** Be subjected to flooding or punishment marketed as “balance.”

## Section 4 — What this book never replaces

This guide teaches **everyday reward-based skills**, stress literacy, and honest stop rules. It does **not** replace:

- Veterinary diagnosis or treatment  
- Behaviour modification for serious aggression, severe anxiety, or welfare crises  
- Legal advice (dangerous dogs, incidents, housing)  
- Multi-dog household plans beyond **management** mentions — separate resources, gates, and professional assessment when fights occur

**Multi-dog homes:** Management — separate feeding, supervised introductions, escape routes — prevents rehearsal of conflict. Training one dog while the other rehearses chaos through a gate is a setup problem, not a moral test.

If you use a companion app, treat it as **practice prompts**, not a clinician. When the app and the book disagree with your dog’s safety, **the dog wins** — simplify, manage, call for help.

## When guilt shows up

Many owners delay the vet or the behaviourist because they fear judgement — from the receptionist, the trainer, or themselves. This book’s position: **asking early is competent**, not weak. A dog who growls at the groomer is communicating; a teenager who pulls is developmentally noisy; a rescue who hides is not “ungrateful.” Guilt is a poor training plan. Use the table, use the mantra, use the phone.

**Red flags that skip the table and go straight to help:** bites with injury; dog cannot eat or rest for extended periods; you fear your own dog; children are at risk; self-harm or violence between dogs in the home. Management (separation, muzzle training with a professional if advised, emergency vet) comes before another chapter.

## Decision sketch (not a flowchart tattoo)

1. **Safe right now?** If no → separate, secure, vet/emergency as needed.  
2. **Sudden or painful?** → Vet.  
3. **Teaching stall only?** → Five checks + easier rep.  
4. **Fear/aggression pattern?** → Reward-based professional with vet involvement.  
5. **Still stuck?** → Second opinion; you are allowed to fire a trainer who frightens your dog.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Knowing when to stop reading and start dialling is **success**.  
- **Do:** Keep your vet number and one professional contact accessible.  
- **Don't:** Delay help because you wanted to finish the book first.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They cannot read the chapter list; they need relief now when suffering.  
- **Do:** Benefit from qualified teams when problems exceed kitchen-scale teaching.  
- **Don't:** Wait until everyone is injured to admit scope.

## Troubleshooting

- **If you cannot tell which check failed** → Assume comfort + context; make setup easier tomorrow; one variable.  
- **If professionals disagree** → Prioritise methods you can live with ethically; second opinion; vet as hub.  
- **If you cannot afford help** → Charities and group classes vary; management remains free — distance, gates, leads.

## Stop if

- Any bite breaking skin, child at risk, or dog unable to rest — **stop** DIY training; secure safety; professional + vet.  
- You are implementing advice that frightens the dog — **stop**, even if you paid for it.  
- You use training to vent anger — **stop**; walk away; try another day or another human helper for you.
