## Opening

You have met the teaching loop, the reward menu, and the owner mantra. Good. Before we ask your dog to come back past squirrels, cope with door chaos, or walk past a pub garden full of crisps, we need one skill that is not a trick: **noticing when the lesson is too much**.

This chapter is about **reading stress**, not diagnosing it. You are not playing vet, behaviourist, or social-media body-language detective. You are learning to see **clusters** of discomfort, take them seriously, and change the plan before frustration — yours or theirs — becomes the training method. Every real-life chapter after this one assumes you will run a **comfort check** whenever life gets loud. Skip this chapter and jump to recall, and you may train through worry and wonder why trust evaporated.

**Bookmark this chapter** if your dog freezes, won’t take food outside, or looks worried on walks. Read it **before** heavy work on come, lead, or doors.

## Section 1 — Start with relaxed

You cannot spot “not okay” until you know what “okay” looks like for **this** dog. So start there.

A relaxed dog usually has:

- **A soft body.** Weight evenly spread, muscles loose, wiggly rather than rigid.  
- **A soft face.** Relaxed eyes (often slightly squinty), ears in their normal resting position, mouth closed loosely or open with a relaxed tongue.  
- **Easy movement.** Wandering, sniffing at their own pace, flopping down to rest.  
- **Normal appetite.** Happy to take a treat, gently.  
- **Easy recovery.** A sudden noise gets a look, then they go back to what they were doing.

Every dog wears relaxed a bit differently. A greyhound at rest looks nothing like a pug at rest. A dog with a curly tail, cropped coat, or long fur over the eyes may be harder to read. That is why your dog’s own **baseline** matters more than any chart.

### Your dog’s baseline card

Spend a quiet evening making a little note. Write down, or take photos of, how your dog looks when they are:

- dozing on the sofa;  
- pottering in the garden;  
- greeting you when you come home;  
- waiting for dinner.

Note tail height, ear position, mouth, how they hold their body. This is your reference picture. When something looks different on a walk, you will have something real to compare it with.

## Section 2 — What stress looks like (clusters, not pixels)

Stress rarely arrives as a single cartoon signal. One yawn might be a tired Tuesday. One lip lick might be anticipation of cheese. What matters is **context plus a cluster**: several signs together, when the situation is genuinely demanding — vet waiting room, crowded pavement, stranger leaning over the garden gate.

Common clusters owners notice at home and on walks include:

- **Body tension:** stiff posture, weight shifted back, tail held low or tight. (Breed matters — compare to *this* dog’s usual loose wiggle.)  
- **Face and mouth:** panting without heat or exercise, a tight closed mouth, whale eye (whites of the eyes showing), repeated lip or nose licks.  
- **Movement choices:** freezing, backing away, trying to leave, hiding behind your legs, suddenly sniffing the ground as if the pavement is fascinating.  
- **Vocalising:** whining, barking that sounds tight rather than playful, growling (information, not insult).  
- **Shut-down:** stillness, refusing food they normally love, “going blank” when touched.

None of these alone proves panic. **Clusters in context** tell you the dog’s nervous system is working overtime. UK welfare guidance consistently warns against training that relies on fear or pain. Noticing stress early is how you stay on the kind side of that line without needing a philosophy lecture mid-walk.

### Excited is not the same as happy

Here is a trap. A very excited dog — bouncing, mouthing, barking, zooming, pulling — can look “happy”. Sometimes they are. But very high excitement and stress use a lot of the same body machinery. A dog who cannot settle, cannot take food gently, and cannot hear you is **over threshold**, whether the feeling underneath is joy or worry.

For training, that means the same thing: too much. The fix is the same too. Lower the excitement. More distance, quieter place, calmer treats placed on the floor rather than thrown, a sniff break. Chapter 9 teaches settling, which helps a lot.

### Growling is a gift

Most owners feel horrified when their dog growls. It is natural to want to tell them off. Please don’t.

A growl is the dog saying, as politely as they can, *“I am not comfortable with this. Please stop.”* If you punish the growl, you do not remove the discomfort. You remove the **warning**. The next time, the dog may skip straight to snapping. That is how “he bit out of nowhere” happens.

When your dog growls: stop what is happening, calmly create space, and make a note of what led up to it. Then think about why. Pain is a common cause, so a vet check is often a sensible first step. If it keeps happening, that is a job for a qualified professional (Chapter 14).

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Stress is **information about the setup**, not a moral failing.  
- **Know:** Believe what you see, even when Auntie insists “he’s fine, he just needs confidence.”  
- **Do:** Name what you notice in plain words — “stiff, won’t eat, backing up”.  
- **Do:** **Pause the lesson.** More distance, quieter street, or full stop.  
- **Don't:** Fixate on one detail from an internet chart.  
- **Don't:** Train “through it” because you paid for a six-week course.  
- **Don't:** Punish growling and call it manners.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Dogs show discomfort long before they bite. Growling and stiffening are **requests for space**, not disrespect.  
- **Do:** Let “I need out” be a valid answer. Move away, end the session, prevent it next time.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that warning signals are ignored until biting is the only option left.

## Section 3 — The stress bucket

A helpful way to picture stress is a **bucket**. Every worrying or exciting thing that happens pours a bit of water in. A loud lorry. A strange dog. A visitor. A vet trip. A bad night’s sleep. Sore paws. Rest, sniffing, and calm let water slowly drain out.

When the bucket overflows, the dog “suddenly” reacts — barks, lunges, snaps, or shuts down. It looks sudden. It rarely is. It is often the fifth thing that day, landing on top of four you did not notice.

This explains a lot of confusing days:

- Why your dog is fine with the neighbour’s dog on Monday and reactive on Friday (Friday came after fireworks, a bath, and a missed walk).  
- Why a dog who copes with visitors at the weekend struggles on Christmas Day (everything at once).  
- Why stress can take **days** to drain, not minutes. After a big fright, many dogs need a few quiet days before they are back to normal.

What to do with it:

- **Notice what fills the bucket** for your dog. Write the big ones down.  
- **Space out the hard things.** Vet on Monday? Don’t plan the busy park on Tuesday.  
- **Build in draining time.** Sniff walks, chews, lick mats, sleep, quiet days.  
- **After a hard day, lower your expectations** for the next one.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** “Out of nowhere” usually means “after lots of things you didn’t see”.  
- **Do:** Plan calm days after stressful ones.  
- **Don't:** Pile a training session on top of a scary event.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** Recovery takes time. Sometimes days.  
- **Do:** Give them easy, calming things to do — sniffing, chewing, licking, sleeping.  
- **Don't:** Ask for hard skills when their bucket is already full.

## Section 4 — Your dog vs the internet chart

Social media loves a single photo with arrows pointing at ears. Real dogs are individuals: breed, age, coat, history, and today’s sleep all change the picture. A rescue who went stiff on day three is not “dominant”. An adolescent who suddenly won’t enter the park may be overwhelmed, not stubborn.

Use charity and vet-trusted explainers as **orientation**, not scorecards. A chart with forty-seven arrows is still not your dog. Useful starting points (check in a browser if a link moves):

- [Blue Cross — pet advice](https://www.bluecross.org.uk/pet-advice) (dog section)  
- [PDSA — canine body language](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/looking-after-your-pet/puppies-dogs/canine-body-language)

Compare your dog **to their own baseline card**. That calm photo is your reference.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Charts help you **ask better questions**. They do not replace your vet or a qualified behaviourist when fear or aggression is frequent.  
- **Do:** Film ten seconds of a tricky moment (safely, from a distance). Watch it without sound. You will often see tension you missed live.  
- **Don't:** Label the dog “anxious” as if it were their identity.  
- **Don't:** Guilt-spiral because a stranger’s collie looks “happier”.  
- **Don't:** Diagnose pain from a blog post.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They are not performing for your camera. They are responding to pressure in the world.  
- **Do:** Build trust by **shrinking the world** until their body loosens again.  
- **Don't:** Flood them with “socialisation” to fix fear.  
- **Don't:** Pair every scary moment with shouted cues.

## Section 5 — Stop, simplify, prevent (comfort in the five checks)

When something stalls, Chapter 4 and Chapter 14 ask you to run **five checks**: setup, reward, ask, **comfort**, place. This chapter owns **comfort**.

If you see a stress cluster:

1. **Stop** — no more goes, no louder recall, no “just one more guest.”  
2. **Simplify** — an easier version of the skill, or give up on teaching for today.  
3. **Prevent** — lead, gate, distance, separate room — so the dog does not rehearse panic while you regroup.

Say the mantra: **“Make it easier — then ask again.”** Sometimes “easier” means **not asking at all** until you have a plan, a vet check, or professional support.

### In the moment, on a walk

You are on the pavement. A big dog appears ahead. Your dog freezes, stares, and the lead goes tight. What now?

1. **Breathe out.** Soften your hand on the lead. Your tension travels down it.  
2. **Make distance.** Cross the road, step into a driveway, or do a cheerful U-turn (“this way!”) and walk away.  
3. **Help them disengage.** Once there is enough space, scatter a few treats on the ground, or ask for an easy hand touch. If they will eat, you are probably far enough away. If they won’t, you need more distance.  
4. **Let them recover.** A slow sniff, a quieter route.  
5. **Note it.** Where, what, how close. That is next week’s plan.

No telling off. No forcing them to “face it”. Just get them to where they can think again.

Comfort is not softness. It is **safety for learning**. A dog who cannot eat chicken on the pavement is telling you the ask or the place is wrong — not that chicken is broken.

### Train yourself

- **Know:** Ending early is a **success**, especially before the real-life chapters.  
- **Know:** Impatience is your cue to shrink the world, not to push.  
- **Do:** Write one line after a hard outing: “Comfort check failed at ___. Next time I will ___.”  
- **Do:** Count the sessions you ended while everyone was still okay.  
- **Don't:** Treat stop rules as defeat.  
- **Don't:** Count minutes drilled.

### Train the dog

- **Know:** They learn whether you are safe to be honest with — or whether they must endure to the end of the walk.  
- **Do:** Let them feel relief when you move away. Make your presence the **way out** of pressure.  
- **Don't:** Let them learn that stress must be ignored to keep you happy.

## Section 6 — When it might be pain

Stress and pain overlap more than people expect. A dog in pain may look worried, snap when touched, stop wanting to walk, stop lying down, or growl when another dog bumps them. Pain is also a very common reason for a dog who was fine to suddenly not be.

See a vet if you notice:

- a sudden change in behaviour, mood, or tolerance;  
- limping, stiffness, slowness getting up, or reluctance to jump;  
- licking or chewing at one spot;  
- flinching or growling when a particular area is touched;  
- changes in eating, drinking, toileting, or sleep;  
- a dog who used to like being stroked and now moves away.

You are not overreacting. Vets would much rather see a dog who turns out to be fine than miss one who isn’t.

## Observation exercise (not a practice lab)

**Five-minute calm watch.** No training, no cues, no phone scroll — yes, we see you. Sit where your dog can choose to be near you or not.

1. Note their baseline: breathing, posture, how they hold tail and ears at rest.  
2. If something changes in the environment (a delivery, a neighbour’s dog, the bin lorry of doom), note **clusters** only.  
3. Note how long it takes them to go back to baseline.  
4. Stop at five minutes.

You are practising **noticing**, not fixing. The dog is not being graded. Do it a few times a week, in different places, and you will be startled by how much more you see.

## Troubleshooting

- **If you only see stress on walks, not at home** → Place (check five). Start practising on quieter routes. Vet check if the change was sudden.  
- **If you are unsure whether it is pain** → Vet first. Limping, flinching on touch, appetite change — not a training problem.  
- **If everyone says “let him work it out”** → Prevention and distance are legitimate tools. Flooding is not this book’s method.  
- **If your dog seems stressed most of the time** → That is too much to fix alone. Vet and a qualified professional (Chapter 14).

## Stop if

- Growling, snapping, biting, or repeated attempts to flee. **Stop.** Make everyone safe. See Chapter 14.  
- You feel yourself speeding up, shouting, or gripping the lead white-knuckled. **Stop.** No dog learns well from that version of you.  
- Stress signs persist daily despite easier setups. Get professional and/or veterinary help, not more practice.
