# Book proposition

**Status:** Living document (Stage B).  
**Language:** British English.

## Working title

**Good Dog: Training the Human** (working title — Option A: subtitle / series frame; adds a wink, not a second syllabus)

The companion app remains [*Good Dog*](https://github.com/GeneralKnowledge/Good-Dog); this book is a standalone work.

## One-line pitch

A definitive, practical guide that teaches ordinary owners how dogs learn, how to train with kindness and clarity, and how to know when management or professional help is the right move — written so you actually want to read it.

## Central argument

Training works best when owners **set the dog up to succeed**, **reward what they want**, **notice discomfort early**, and **match difficulty to the real world** — not when they hunt for a single trick or dominate a frightened animal. Management, veterinary care, and qualified behaviour support are part of good training, not admissions of failure.

## The book’s method

Three linked habits run through the whole book:

### 1. The teaching loop (everyday training)

**Setup → cue → behaviour → mark/reward → release → reset.**

Use this for teaching any skill. Part II (especially ch04) installs the loop; Part III applies it to real life.

### 2. The five checks (when something stalls)

Before repeating a cue louder or assuming the dog is “being difficult”, check:

1. **Setup** — Is the environment small and calm enough?  
2. **Reward** — Does this dog want what you’re offering right now?  
3. **Criterion** — Are you asking for one clear thing, not three at once?  
4. **Comfort** — Any stress signs (clusters, not one pixel on a chart)? Pause or simplify.  
5. **Context** — Did it work at home but fail on the pavement? **Shrink the world** until success is likely again, then grow slowly.

“**Shrink the world**” is plain English for lowering distraction and difficulty until the dog can succeed — then generalise in modest steps. ch06 and ch14 return to this when progress wobbles.

ch01 frames the **fair contract** (clarity, kindness, honest stop); the **loop and checks** are what owners *do*. ch12 deepens **comfort** and reading stress without playing vet.

### 3. Dual track — train yourself / train the dog

For each topic (skill, habit, or problem slice), the book separates **your** job from **the dog’s** job in the same scene:

| Track | Know | Do | Don't |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Train yourself** | What you need to understand first (setup, timing, when to manage) | What you actually do with hands, voice, environment, rewards | Owner habits that wreck progress (repeat cue, train through freeze, etc.) |
| **Train the dog** | What the dog is learning (association, not “obedience theatre”) | The behaviour/criterion you’re building *this session* | Flooding, muddy criteria, poisoned cues — mistakes *you* avoid on their behalf |

Use the headings **Train yourself** and **Train the dog** in the manuscript. Full drafting rules: [`writing/dual-track-pattern.md`](writing/dual-track-pattern.md). ch01 introduces the frame; ch04 installs it on every major block.

## Training the Human (wit + habit)

**Training the Human** is the book’s gentle joke: in most kitchens the dog learns faster than the person with the treat bag. The subtitle is there for **humour and wit** — self-aware, warm, never sneering at a struggling owner.

Under the joke sits a real habit: dogs learn through consequences; so do owners. When you adjust setup, reward, criteria, or context, you are doing the same kind of learning you ask of your dog: clear cues, fair pay, honest stops.

**Tagline (comic, sparing):** *Train the human first.*

### Owner mantra (repeat when stuck)

**“Make it easier — then ask again.”**

Say it before you repeat a cue louder or blame the dog. It maps to the five checks: easier setup/reward/criterion, watch comfort, shrink the world. Optional human-marker habit: a quiet **“Good human.”** when *you* fix the plan — not when the dog fails.

ch04 installs the mantra alongside the teaching loop; ch06 and ch14 call it back when progress wobbles. The mantra is a **pedagogical device**, not medical or behavioural diagnosis.

## Intended reader

**Primary:** Ordinary dog owners who want to raise or live successfully with a dog and are not professional trainers. Accessible to first-time owners; still useful to experienced owners who want clearer behaviour literacy and better troubleshooting.

**Life stages:** Puppies, adolescents, adults, and rescue dogs — with explicit notes that history, health, and temperament differ.

## Practical promise

After reading, a motivated owner should be able to:

- Explain, in plain language, how reward-based learning works  
- Run the **teaching loop** and the **five checks** when training stalls or context changes  
- Choose rewards, timing, and session length sensibly  
- Teach foundations: engagement, settle, recall basics, comfortable lead walking, handling, home manners  
- Read stress signals with appropriate uncertainty (context, not single-cue diagnosis)  
- Respond to setbacks without blame or escalation  
- Recognise when to manage, call the vet, or seek a qualified reward-based professional  

## Voice and tone

- Warm, witty, conversational, memorable  
- Intelligent without academic fog; practical without dullness  
- Compassionate without sentimentality or preaching  
- Serious about welfare; not solemn about ourselves  

**Humour:** Recognisable owner experiences, precise observation, understatement, everyday absurdity with a dog in the house. Never the dog as butt of cruelty; never mock distressed owners; never trivialise fear, pain, aggression, or welfare.

**Originality:** Develop an authorial voice for this book. Do not imitate another writer’s distinctive prose.

## Training and welfare position

- Reward-based training and welfare-centred handling as the default  
- No pain, intimidation, forced exposure, or “dominance/submission” framing as training method  
- No implication that reward-based training makes every problem easy or fast  
- Distinguish training, management, environmental change, veterinary input, and specialist behaviour support  
- Avoid universal guarantees; explain individual factors and boundaries  

## Editorial ambition

More than a manual: a coherent reading journey with a clear through-line, realistic scenarios, actionable steps, observable success criteria, troubleshooting for common failure modes, minimal jargon (introduced in context), and minimal repetition across chapters.
